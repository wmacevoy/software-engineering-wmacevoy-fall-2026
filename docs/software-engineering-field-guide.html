Part I

The discipline

Why we work this way: what engineers are for, and what most of the practice is actually managing.

1 Why We Work This Way

What this course thinks the discipline is, and why the rest of the repository is shaped the way it is.

Nothing in this file is checkable by continuous integration. That is exactly why it has to be written down: the parts of engineering that no tool enforces are the parts that get lost, and then rediscovered at full price.

1.1 What engineers are for

Engineers make reliable things, on time, within resource constraints, in a way that manages discomfort for the team, the client, and the users.

Four constraints that trade against each other, and a fifth — scope — that is usually the honest one to move. A plan that does not say which of them is fixed is not a plan; it is a wish with dates attached.

Note that reliable is not correct. Correctness is a property of a program measured against a specification. Reliability is a property of a system running in a world that contains bad inputs, hostile inputs, failing hardware, wrong clocks, partial networks, and people. The gap between those two words is where most of the work is, and parking/ is built almost entirely inside it.

Discomfort is a first-class variable

WhoseWhat it looks likeWhat reduces it
The usersconfusion, wasted time, distrust of the answerthe product actually being good
The clientsurprisefrequent, honest, boring status
The teamfear, thrash, heroics, weekendsslack, small steps, reversible decisions

This is not about comfort as a goal. Fear degrades judgment, and a frightened team abandons exactly the practices that would have prevented the fear. Discomfort is a leading indicator — usually the earliest one available, arriving weeks before the schedule shows anything.

1.2 Why software engineering differs

Software engineering has two advantages the older disciplines do not, and between them they account for most of what follows:

  • Tests are cheap. You cannot build a second bridge to find out whether the first one holds. You can run a suite a thousand times before lunch. What follows from that is "The cost of testing changes everything".
  • Software has no gravity. Nothing sags or cracks to tell you it is wrong, so every signal a physical structure gives away for free has to be built here on purpose. What follows from that is "Nothing has mass".

The two pull in opposite directions, which is why neither is simply good news. Cheap tests make certainty affordable; the absence of gravity is what makes it necessary to go buy some, because a bad design will hold itself up indefinitely, at full speed, with the tests passing.

1.3 Most of practice is being less nervous

Backups. Version control. Tests. Code review. Short iterations. Client demos. Staging environments. Each is normally taught as a rule to follow. Each is better understood as a way of retiring one specific fear, and you should be able to name which.

PracticeThe fear it retires
Backups"I could lose the work."
Version control"I can't get back to when it worked."
Tests"I don't know if I just broke something."
Continuous integration"It works on my machine."
Short iterations"We are building the wrong thing."
Client demos"They will hate it in month six."
Code review"Only one person understands this."
Signed commits"Nobody vouched for this code."
Fault injection"I don't know what happens when it breaks."

Two consequences follow, and both are load-bearing:

  • A practice that reduces no one's anxiety will be abandoned under pressure. Ceremony that costs time and returns no relief gets dropped in week ten, and it should be. If you cannot name the fear a practice retires, either find it or stop doing it.
  • A practice that reduces anxiety without reducing risk is worse than nothing. A backup nobody has restored. A test suite that cannot fail. A green dashboard measuring the wrong thing. These are the most dangerous artifacts in engineering, because they spend the alarm and leave the danger in place.

1.4 The taxonomy of unknowing

#CategoryWhat it is
K0The knownThings you know, that are true.
K1The known unknownThings you know you don't know.
K2The unknown unknownThings you didn't know you didn't know.
K3Erroneous certaintyWhat feels like K0 and is false.
K4True falsehoodsThings we hold as true, knowing they aren't, because they're useful.

K0 through K3 are in order of expense. Looking something up is cheap. Researching a question you have already framed is more. Being surprised costs a rewrite. Being confidently wrong costs whatever was built on top of the belief, discovered at the worst possible moment. K4 is not on that ladder at all — it is a choice, and a good one.

Building something is the act of discovering, in the right order and at the right price, which category each part of a problem domain belongs to. Every practice in this repository is a way of moving something down the ladder before it charges full price.

FromToHow
K0staysWrite it down so it stays known: tests are executable knowledge, and unlike prose they complain when they go stale.
K1K0Spikes, prototypes, reading the source, asking the client. Cheap, and available on demand — the only category you can simply decide to spend money on.
K2K1Exposure to reality, early and repeatedly: integrate, deploy, demo, inject faults, watch real use. You cannot search for these; you can only arrange to be surprised sooner and more cheaply.
K3K0Evidence: a test that could have failed, an output you actually looked at, a signature you checked, a second person who does not share your assumption.
K4chosenName the falsehood, write down where it is load-bearing, know the blast radius when it comes due.

K3 is the one to be afraid of

K1 and K2 are honest. K3 is expensive precisely because it costs you nothing at the time — no search is triggered, no question is asked, no ticket is filed. You are not looking, because as far as you know there is nothing to look for.

Only two things find K3: reality, and someone who does not share your assumption. That is the entire justification for running the code instead of reasoning about it, for integrating early, for review by a person rather than by yourself an hour later, and for F-6, which requires every attack to be demonstrated before its defense is built. "I verified the signature" is K3 until you have personally forged a reading and watched the check reject it.

K4: the falsehoods we keep on purpose

Every abstraction is a chosen falsehood. parking/ holds a stack of them, and each is written down somewhere in REQUIREMENTS.md precisely because it is false:

  • The clock is correct. (N-2, N-5, and the skewed fault say otherwise.)
  • The network delivers. (C-1 exists because it doesn't.)
  • The sensor is honest. (F is a catalogue of ways it isn't.)
  • Capacity is constant. (S-6: a mismatch is a fault, not a correction.)
  • The token reflects the user's current role. (It reflects their role up to eight hours ago.)

The engineering act is not avoiding these — you cannot build anything without them. It is choosing them deliberately, recording them, and knowing what breaks when one comes due. An abstraction leaks exactly where its falsehood was load-bearing. A K4 you have chosen is an asset; the same falsehood held unknowingly is K3.

1.5 Time

Tie a string to a rat that has learned one side of the cage is electrified, and measure how hard it pulls away as it is drawn toward that side. The pull is not linear. It is nearly flat far out and rises steeply as the distance closes — an avoidance gradient, roughly 1/x in shape.

Deadlines do this to people. The month before ship feels like nothing; the last week is unbearable. The exact curve is a metaphor, not a measurement, but the shape is the part that matters, and it has a vicious property: the spike arrives exactly when the project can least afford the behavior it produces. Skipped tests, bundled diffs, unreviewed merges, "we'll clean it up after launch." The fear near the deadline destroys the practices that would have made the deadline survivable. That feedback loop, not incompetence, is how most projects actually fail.

The engineering response is not willpower. It is to change the terrain.

  • Remove the danger. If deploying is routine and reversible, the shore stops being electrified. An always-releasable trunk means shipping is not an event.
  • Build the bridge. A thin end-to-end slice — click to route to SQL to response — built early and then thickened, crosses the gap. Horizontal layers all finish at the same time: never.
  • Put small fences along the way. Intermediate checkpoints flatten the curve, but only if they are real. A demo to an actual client, a conformance run that exits nonzero, a deploy that happened. A status meeting is not a fence; it discharges no fear, because nothing was proved.
  • Keep slack. A plan at full utilization has no capacity to absorb K2, and K2 is guaranteed. Queues do not degrade gracefully near full utilization; latency goes to infinity. Slack is not waste, it is the budget line for the unknown unknown.
  • Estimate the category, not the task. "Two days" usually means "two days if nothing I haven't thought of happens" — a K0 statement about a schedule governed by K2. Work sitting in K0 estimates well. K1 work estimates as a range. K2 work cannot be estimated at all, only time-boxed. Say which kind you are handing over.
  • Bad news early is a gift; bad news late is a betrayal. A two-week slip found in week two is a planning problem. The same slip found in week eleven is a crisis. The delay in reporting, not the slip, is what makes it expensive — and hiding it is the most common way engineers create the client discomfort they were trying to avoid.

1.6 The cost of testing changes everything

The first structural difference between software engineering and the other engineering disciplines is the cost of a test. You cannot build a second bridge to see whether the first one stands. A wind tunnel hour is expensive, a mask set is expensive, a recall is catastrophic. So those disciplines front-load everything — analysis, simulation, review, sign-off — before anything is cut. Waterfall is not a bad habit inherited from the 1970s; it is the correct response to a test that costs a million dollars and takes a year.

Software's tests can be free, run in a second, and run ten thousand times a day. Nearly everything filed under "agile" is downstream of that single fact. Three things follow that are usually left out:

1. It is per decision, not per project. Both regimes live inside this one repository. Changing a route handler is seconds to verify. Changing a schema under a database with real data in it is not (V-7). A released firmware image cannot be recalled (G). A private key that has leaked cannot be unleaked (I-2, I-7). A published interface with users on it is a promise (V-4). So you run waterfall locally, around the expensive-to-test decisions — think it through, review it, get it right the first time — and agile around the cheap ones. Methodology is a function of the cost of being wrong once, and that cost can change twice in an afternoon.

2. The cost of a test is designed, not given. Lowering it is ordinary engineering work with a known toolkit: pure functions, deterministic seeds (T-1), faults driven through a control plane instead of waited for (F-1, T-2), one command back to a known state (T-5), conformance checkable from outside (T-4), and logs that record the inputs that drove a decision so a failure explains itself (C-9). Lowering the cost of a test is often the highest-leverage work available, because it multiplies against every future change. And the inverse is diagnostic: if something is hard to test, that is information about the design, not about testing.

3. Testing is one feedback loop among several. Each has a cost, a latency, and a fidelity, and they catch different classes of error:

LoopLatencyCatches
Running the thingseconds"it doesn't work at all"
Unit testsecondsbroken logic, regressions
Integration testminuteswrong assumptions between components
Human reviewhoursunclear intent, missing cases, bad design
Client demodaysbuilding the wrong product
Production telemetryliveeverything you didn't imagine — at the worst possible price

Push each class of error down to the cheapest, fastest loop that can catch it. But notice that some errors are only catchable by an expensive loop: no test suite in the world detects "we built the wrong thing." Only a person looking at it does.

Two properties of a loop matter more than its coverage:

  • A test that cannot fail proves nothing. Watch it fail first — which is why CONTRIBUTING.md requires that the test fail without the change. A test written after the fact, never observed red, is a K3 generator with a green checkmark.
  • A loop you do not trust is worse than none. Flaky tests train a team to ignore red, and after that the suite that would have caught the real one has been disarmed. T-1 bans unseeded randomness for this reason, not for tidiness.

1.7 Nothing has mass

The second difference is physical, and it shows up first in the schedule.

Drywall goes up after plumbing. Not by convention — the pipe occupies the space the wall will cover, and matter cannot be threaded through matter after the fact. The frame follows the foundation because it rests on it. Every arrow in a Gantt chart in the older disciplines is, at bottom, a fact about mass: one thing must physically exist before the next can be attached to it, and the half-finished intermediate states are real objects that cost real money.

Software is massless. A function can be called before it is written. Nothing occupies the space another thing will need. There is no partially-poured anything.

So when a software schedule says "we can't start B until A is done," that arrow is almost never a fact about the world. It is a fact about the design: B does not know what A will look like. The dependency is an unwritten interface, and it is being paid for in calendar time.

The design goal is a flat chart — everything startable at once. The mechanism is one the discipline already owns, used deliberately instead of incidentally:

The dependency saysThe artifact that dissolves it
"B needs A's shape"an interface — signature, schema, error codes
"B needs A to answer"a mock that answers, and misbehaves on request
"B isn't done until A works"a test that fails today and passes when A arrives

None of the three requires A to exist. The dependency does not get sequenced, it gets specified — which is why contract/ is layer 0 and was built first while seven of the components that will use it are still only READMEs. The promise is what unblocks, not the implementation. collector/ gets a folder while running in-process for the same reason: so that the interface is real before the network is.

The cost is real and has to be paid on purpose. A mock is a K4 — the real component will behave like this — false the day you write it. Flattening the chart does not delete the dependency; it converts a scheduling risk into an integration risk. That is a good trade only if you integrate continuously. Mock everything, integrate at the end, and you have built the worst artifact in this document: every team delivered on time and the system does not work. That is the Time section's horizontal-layers failure arriving through a side door.

Two rules keep the trade good:

  • The mock and the real thing must be interchangeable under the same tests. That is what T-4 is for — conformance checkable from outside means the fake and the shipped sensor answer to one specification, so swapping them is an event with a result rather than a discovery.
  • Sequence around irreversibility, not around incompleteness. The orderings that genuinely survive in software are the one-way doors — a schema with data under it (V-7), a distributed key (I-2), a released image (G-3) — plus the honest case where you must learn something before you can design against it, and a spike is the mock for that. Every other arrow in the chart is a confession that an interface has not been written down.

One warning follows from the same property. Mass supplies error signals for free: a bad structure sags, cracks, or falls down, and nobody has to be clever to notice. Software has no gravity, so nothing collapses to tell you. A bad design holds itself up indefinitely, at full speed, with the tests passing. Every feedback loop in the previous section exists to replace the signal that mass would have given away.

1.8 Failure is an input, not an exception

Most specifications describe the happy path and inherit the rest by accident — whatever the HTTP library happens to do on timeout becomes the product's behavior, chosen by nobody.

For every input a component depends on, four cases need a stated answer: absent, late, wrong, and lying. parking/ exists to force those four questions on every consumer, which is why the sensor is a designated liar with a switchboard.

A system that only works when its inputs behave is not finished. And an adversary is just the unknown that optimizes: security is this same discipline applied to an input chosen specifically to find your K3.

1.9 Interfaces, reversibility, and the installed base

  • Spend deliberation in proportion to reversibility, not to how interesting the decision is. Renaming a variable is free to undo. A data format with data already in it, a published URL, a distributed key, an artifact someone installed — those are one-way doors. Most arguments in software are conducted at the wrong intensity for their reversibility.
  • The moment a second party depends on your interface, it stops being a decision and becomes a promise. V-4 is that idea made precise, and versioning (V-1–V-5) is the mechanism for keeping the promise while still being allowed to change.
  • The property being engineered is the cost of the next change. Anyone can write the first version. The design shows its worth in the second, which is also when nobody remembers why the first was shaped that way — so write that down while you still know.

1.10 Provenance

Every claim in a system has an author, and the question that runs through this entire repository is: who said this, and can I check it later without asking them again?

It is the same question at every layer — a reading (S-8, I-5), a log record (L-9), a firmware image (G-2), a commit (CONTRIBUTING.md). The device registry and .github/allowed_signers are the same structure one layer apart, and noticing that is the point.

Provenance is the mechanical answer to K3. "I'm sure this is right" is a feeling. "This is signed by a key in the registry, and here is the check" is evidence. And on the human side, a signature is a person saying I have read this and I stand behind it — which cannot be delegated, and which the arrival of capable agents makes more important rather than less.

1.11 Scale, recovery, availability

Three questions the system answers at runtime whether or not anyone wrote them down. Unstated, the answer is whatever the implementation happened to do — chosen by nobody, and discovered in production.

RequirementThe question that forces itAn answer looks like
ScaleHow many actual users? And lots, readings a second, rows in a year?an order of magnitude, plus a horizon
RecoveryWhat happens when X breaks — and what does it take to get back?a procedure someone has run and timed
AvailabilityHow much downtime is tolerable, for whom, per what period?a number with a period

Scale. "A city" is not a number. Eight lots, one sensor each, a reading every thirty seconds, twenty thousand drivers on a Friday evening, ninety days of history — those are design inputs; the first is a mood. Precision is not the point; the order of magnitude is, and so is everyone holding the same one. Postgres and a five-second poll is the obviously right answer at ten lots and an obviously wrong one at a million sensors, and nothing about the code says which world it is in. Ask for the number and its horizon — "and in two years?" — then write down the number you are explicitly not designing for, which is the cheapest scope decision available and ends the same argument permanently. The number is K4: false the day you write it. Written down it is an asset with a known blast radius; assumed, it is the K3 that rewrites get named after.

Recovery. "What happens when X breaks" is two questions, and the second is the one that gets skipped. First: what does the system do while X is down — degrade, wait, refuse, serve stale, and does the user learn which? That is what C and U-1 are for. Second: what does it take to get back — who does it, with which command, from which backup, and how long does it take? That answer is real only if someone has run it and timed it; a restore procedure nobody has executed is the canonical anxiety-reducing artifact with the risk left in place. Answer both per component, not for "the system": the answer for a sensor (poll it again in five seconds) is nothing like the answer for the database (restore, and lose everything since the last backup — how much is that, in minutes?). That last figure is itself a requirement, not a consequence: how much data may we lose has to be decided by someone entitled to decide it.

Availability. Downtime tolerance is a budget with a period and a beneficiary: the driver-facing page may be down four hours a month; the ops UI may be down a day; a single missed poll bothers nobody. Different components deserve different budgets — that is exactly what lets you spend the money where it matters. Two things make this worth forcing early:

  • The unstated default is 100%, which is the most expensive requirement you can accidentally accept, and it quietly forbids deploys, restarts, and schema migrations (V-7). A team that never gave itself a maintenance budget cannot do the "remove the danger" move from the Time section, because every release is an incident.
  • The number chooses the architecture — mostly by choosing whether a human is allowed in the loop at all:
BudgetDown per monthWhat it implies
99%7 hoursa person can notice and fix it
99.9%43 minutesalerting that pages, and a runbook someone has run
99.99%4 minutesno human in the loop: redundancy and automatic failover

None of these three is ours to choose alone — they are client answers, and on day one each costs one sentence. Later they cost a migration, a rewrite, or an outage, in that order.

1.12 Security, ethics, and law are requirements

Three more permanent members of the requirement set, and the easiest of all to postpone: no user files a ticket for them, they compete against visible features, and their absence looks exactly like their presence right up until it doesn't.

Two properties make deferring them a mistake rather than a trade-off:

  • Their deadline is set by someone else — a breach, an audit, a complaint, a regulator, a journalist. You do not get to schedule it into the next sprint.
  • Violations are the irreversible kind. Data that leaked cannot be unleaked. A person harmed is not fixed by a patch. These are one-way doors, and the earlier section applies at full force.

In practice:

  • Security. Write down the threat model: who the adversary is, what they can do, what they want. Then design against that, not against a vague sense of danger. Every dependency is a transfer of trust and a permanent obligation; know where you have placed it and why.
  • Law. Mostly about data — what you collect, how long you keep it, who you must notify, what you may reuse. Also licensing and the provenance of code, which now includes code an agent produced. Note that A-5 puts retention deliberately outside the reach of any API: that is a legal design as much as a security one.
  • Ethics. The people affected who are not in the room — non-users, bystanders, the surveilled. Parking data is location data about people. "Counts, not locations" in REQUIREMENTS.md is an ethical decision wearing technical clothes: it is nearly free to make now and nearly impossible to retrofit later. Data minimization is the cheapest protection that exists — the safest record is the one you never kept. Accessibility and translation live here too (eight language packs are not decoration), as does the question of what the system does to the person whose lot shows the wrong number.

The cheapest moment to satisfy all three is while the requirements are still soft. After that you are not adding a requirement, you are performing a migration.

1.13 People are part of the system

  • Coordination cost grows faster than the team. Adding people to a late project makes it later. The fix is smaller, clearer interfaces between people — not more meetings.
  • The system will resemble the communication structure that built it. If that is going to happen anyway, choose the structure on purpose.
  • The deliverable is not the code. It is the code plus the ability of someone else to run it, reset it, deploy it, diagnose it at 3am, and continue it in month seven. "It works on my machine" is a claim about a machine.
  • Maintenance is most of the total cost, and the reader you are writing for is most often yourself, having forgotten. This is why AGENTS.md treats attention as the scarce resource.
  • Write down what you decided not to do. The Scope decisions and Non-goals sections of REQUIREMENTS.md are the cheapest documentation in this repository: they end the same argument three times and tell the future why the obvious thing was not done.
  • Distribute discomfort deliberately and visibly — on-call, review load, the tedious work. Burnout is a systems failure with a schedule, not a personal failing, and it shows up as attrition, which is the uncontrolled deletion of K0 with no backup.

1.14 Working with agents

An agent is an extremely fast writer with no stake in the outcome. It moves the economics of this discipline in exactly one direction: the cost of producing code has fallen much faster than the cost of knowing that the code is right. Everything scarce therefore migrates to the second half — specification, verification, review, ownership.

In the taxonomy, an agent is a K3 factory. Fluent, confident, plausible, and occasionally wrong is the precise shape of erroneous certainty, now arriving faster than you can check it. That is not an argument against using agents. It is an argument about where to spend the time they save.

  • Spend the speed on lowering the cost of testing — fixtures, harnesses, seed data, fault scripts, conformance checks, the boring second test case. That work multiplies. More features do not.
  • Review capacity is now the bottleneck. Ask for diffs you can actually read, one concern at a time.
  • Never let confidence substitute for a run. "This should work" is K3 by construction.
  • Delegation is not abdication. You sign it, so you own it — and if you cannot explain it, you cannot maintain it or defend it. Working code you cannot explain is a liability in this repository, not an asset.

Nothing important may live only in a head

A design constraint that exists only in a developer's head has always been a liability. It used to be a survivable one: the person holding it sat nearby, saw the change, and said "no, not like that" in the hallway. With agents in the loop that recovery path is gone. An agent is not in the hallway, does not know the constraint exists, and will hand you a clean, tested, plausible violation of it faster than you can read the diff.

So the state of the project has to be observable from where every party stands — the client, the humans, and each agent, which arrives knowing nothing and leaves remembering nothing. An unwritten constraint fails in both directions at once: the holder's belief that everyone knows it is K3, and for everyone else it is K2 — you cannot ask about something you have no idea exists.

The working set

What "observable" requires will keep changing as the tools do. Today it means four documents, in the repository, versioned alongside the code — not in a wiki, not in chat history, not in a notebook:

DocumentAnswersWritten by

  • _vX, because a superseded requirement is not deleted knowledge. Editing in place destroys the before-picture, and the diff between two versions is the most useful conversation the client ever has with you. Same idea as V-4 one layer up: once a second party depends on a statement, changing it is a version, not an edit.
  • QUEUE is the shared cursor. Without one, two workers do the same task twice, or each assumes the other has it. That failure was always possible with people; it is now cheap to hit ten times in an hour.
  • FINDINGS is the K2/K3 ledger, and the only one of the four that reality writes. A fact discovered at full price and not written down gets rediscovered at full price. An agent has no memory of last week's dead end — and increasingly, neither do you.

These are not project-management ceremony. Each retires a specific fear — "we are building different things," "nobody knows what state this is in," "we already learned this once" — and by the earlier rule, a document that retires no fear should be dropped rather than maintained.

Context is a budget, and it resets every session

An agent starts from nothing, every time. On a real system it cannot read the project to find out what is going on: it does not fit, and even where it fits, reading everything in order to change one thing is the wrong price. So how does a competent stranger find the five files that matter, in ten minutes, without reading the other five hundred? stops being a nicety and becomes a load-bearing property of the repository.

The good news is that this is the same property that makes a codebase habitable for people. Clear component boundaries, a README per component stating its contract, stable IDs like S-8 that grep from a test back to the sentence that demanded it, a dependency rule that can be checked mechanically, names that mean what they say — humans navigate by these, and so do agents. Factoring done for human comprehension pays twice.

The bad news is that several conventions that genuinely helped human readers now cost real money:

ConventionWhat it costs now
A doc-comment on every functionSpends context restating what the signature already says — and prose goes stale silently, where stale prose reads exactly like true prose. K3, pre-delivered.
One enormous fileCannot be read in part, so every task pays for all of it.
Deep indirection in the name of reuseThe seam-hiding tax, charged to humans and agents alike.
Commented-out code, historical asidesAmbiguous authority: is this the specification, or the past?

The replacement is not "no documentation." It is self-documenting code, a hint exactly where the code cannot say why, and everything durable in the working set, where both kinds of reader look. That is the rule AGENTS.md already gives for comments; agents did not change it, they only put a price tag on breaking it.

Ask your agents

Models are changing faster than this document can. Any specific advice about how to prompt, how much to hand over at once, or what an agent can be trusted to do unsupervised has a short shelf life — the conventions just above included.

The durable move is to ask the model you are actually using: what did you have to read that you should not have needed? what would have let you get this right the first time? what did you assume because nobody wrote it down? Then put the answer in the repository rather than the chat, where the next session — human or not — will find it. Treat it as a proposal from something with no stake in the outcome, like any other advice here. But ask, because the alternative is a convention tuned for a model that has already been retired.

2 Agile and Waterfall

The shape of software engineering: two orders for the same six steps, the one fact that decides between them, the words people use, and the failure that looks exactly like progress.

2.2 The same six steps

Both shapes are made of the same six steps, and the steps are in the right order in both. You cannot design against a requirement you do not have; you cannot build a design you have not chosen; you cannot deploy what you have not built.

StepWhat it producesThe question it answers

The disagreement between the two shapes is not about the order. It is about the batch size and about where the tests are.

2.3 Waterfall, described

Waterfall runs the six steps once, in order, each finished and signed off before the next begins:

Requirements → Design → Develop → Test → Deploy → Maintain

Winston Royce drew it in 1970 and, two pages later, wrote that the implementation as drawn "is risky and invites failure," because testing arrives at the end, where every surprise costs the most. The industry kept the drawing and dropped the warning.

It is worth being fair to the shape, because outside software it is correct. A civil engineer cannot build a second bridge to see whether the first one stands. A chip designer pays for a mask set once. When the only real test costs a million dollars and a year, the rational response is to move everything that can be checked before that test: analysis, simulation, review, sign-off, change control, a requirements document that is frozen because changing it means re-doing the analysis. Waterfall is what a discipline looks like when it cannot afford to be wrong twice.

What waterfall retiresWhat it creates
"we will spend the money on a design that was never checked""we will find out it was the wrong product at the end, at full price"
"the requirements moved under us""the requirements were frozen before anyone had seen the thing"
"nobody signed off""everyone signed off, and it was still wrong"

The right-hand column is what happens when the shape is applied to work whose tests are cheap. K2 — the surprise you could not have asked about — is guaranteed on every project, and waterfall schedules its arrival for the last step.

2.4 Agile, described

Agile runs the same steps in loops, with a test inside every step rather than a Test step at the end. Three loops, three latencies, three different instruments:

Release loop       (weeks; reality answers)
  Iteration loop   (days; a person answers; time-boxed, and scope is what moves)
    Requirements   (test: the client reads a precise claim and objects; K4s read back)
    Design         (test: a contract, a mock that misbehaves, a spike, a failing test)
    Develop        (test: red, then green — the seconds loop)
    Demo           (test: a person drives the real thing)              ← the fence
  Deploy           (test: health-gated, automatic revert, rollback timed)
  Observe          (production telemetry; what K2 arrived → FINDINGS)
  Delete           (what can we remove?)
There is no Maintain step. After the first deploy, every iteration is maintenance.

Five things about the shape are load-bearing:

  • "(test)" means something different at each step. Requirements are tested by a person — the client reading a sentence precise enough to be wrong. Design is tested by artifacts that exist before the code: a contract two people can build against, a mock that misbehaves on request, a spike, a test that fails today. Develop is tested by red then green. Deploy is tested by a health check with a timeout and an automatic revert. Leave the label off and students will hear "unit test" four times. TESTING.md spells each one out.
  • The iteration ends on a fence, not on "done." A fixed period — one to two weeks — that ends with a running thing in front of a human. When the period ends, scope is what moves. Without the time box the inner loop is not a loop, it is a spiral.
  • The demo is the only instrument for "wrong product." No test suite detects that you built exactly what was asked and nobody uses it. A person driving the real thing does, and that is why the demo closes the inner loop rather than decorating it.
  • The outer loop's product is knowledge. Deploy answers "does it survive reality"; Observe writes down what reality said (FINDINGS); Delete returns the carry nobody schedules. The distinctive output of agile is not more code sooner. It is K2 converted to K1 at the cheapest available price, in the right order.
  • Maintain is not a step because it is the loop. Waterfall drew maintenance last and smallest and put most of the money there. Agile has no line between building and maintaining: the second iteration after the first deploy is the same shape as the first.

2.5 The distinction is the cost of a test

Everything in section 2.3 is legal for one reason: a test can be free, run in a second, and run ten thousand times a day. That is what lets Requirements be wrong on Monday and corrected on Tuesday, lets a design be a contract and a mock instead of a signed-off drawing, and lets Deploy be routine instead of an event.

So the choice between the shapes is not a preference. It is determined, per decision, by the cost of being wrong once — and both regimes live inside one repository:

DecisionCost of being wrong onceRegime

Read the right-hand column top to bottom and notice that the regime changes as the door becomes harder to reverse. Around a one-way door you run a small waterfall inside the loop: get the requirement exactly, design it on paper, review it with someone who does not share your assumption, and do it once. Everywhere else you loop. Methodology is a function of the cost of being wrong once, and that cost can change twice in an afternoon.

The last row is the subject of the next section, because it is the door students walk through without noticing there was a door.

2.6 Falling down the waterfall

Here is the failure, and it looks like a productive week.

A team is asked for a campus parking app. By Tuesday there is a schema: students(id, name, plate), vehicles(plate, student_id), sightings(plate, lot, seen_at), third normal form, an index on (lot, seen_at), a view that joins all three, a query that answers "where did this student park last Thursday" in eleven milliseconds. It is good work. Everyone is comfortable.

In week four the client is asked — late, in passing, per the intake question about who is affected who is not in the room — whether student identity may be joined to a location. The registrar's answer is no: not without consent, and consent is not on the table. Whether the rule is the federal privacy law for student records, the institution's own policy, or a data-protection law that applies because one user is abroad does not matter to the schema. What matters is that sightings cannot exist in the form it has, the join is forbidden, the view is forbidden, the query is forbidden, and the user interface built on the view has no data. The whole plan was misguided by a rule that lives at the top of the waterfall, and the team spent four weeks at the bottom.

parking/ carries the one-line requirement that would have saved the month: "Counts, not locations." It is in Scope decisions, it has an ethical reason attached in SOFTWARE-ENGINEERING.md, and no amount of database normalization would ever have produced it. It comes from a question, not from a design.

Why the slope is there

Software has no mass, so this is not gravity. It is the comfort gradient. Each step down the waterfall is more concrete, more familiar, more solitary, and produces something you can see: a table, a query, a screen. Each step up is more ambiguous, more social, and produces a document nobody grades. Reading a privacy law is uncomfortable. Asking a registrar a question is uncomfortable. Writing a sentence precise enough for a client to reject is uncomfortable. Normalizing a schema is a pleasure.

SOFTWARE-ENGINEERING.md makes discomfort a first-class variable and says that people abandon the practices that would have saved them at exactly the moment the fear peaks. This is the same mechanism earlier in the project: people go where their own discomfort is lowest, which is the bottom of the waterfall, and leave the client's and the users' discomfort — surprise, and a product that cannot legally exist — for later. The slope of the waterfall is the developer's comfort gradient, drawn as a diagram.

Agents make it steeper. An agent will produce the normalized schema, the indexes, and fifty queries in a minute, and it will never ask about the registrar unless a requirement says to. It falls down the waterfall at machine speed.

The tells

You are falling when any of these is true before there is a numbered requirement the client has objected to:

  • There is a schema.
  • There is a framework, a language, or a library chosen.
  • There is a "temporary" table, column, or default.
  • "We'll figure out login later." "We'll add the privacy stuff at the end."
  • There is no Non-goals list, so nothing has been refused yet.
  • Nobody can say who, outside the room, this system keeps a record about.

The railing

The engineering response is the one the essay gives for fear: do not rely on willpower, change the terrain. Put fences at the top of the waterfall so that descending early is visibly a violation rather than an instinct.

  1. Ask the expensive questions first. The questions whose answer could invalidate the plan come before the questions whose answer only shapes it. What data about people does this keep? Who is entitled to see it? What law or policy applies, and who says so? How many, and in two years? How long may it be down? What are we not building? Each is a sentence to ask and a month to discover. docs/TEAM-AND-CLIENT.md has the full list; this is the subset with a regulator attached.
  2. Write the answer where it cannot be missed. Scope decisions, Non-goals, and a section of the requirements for security, law, and ethics — three permanent members of the requirement set, as the essay says, with deadlines set by someone else.
  3. Read the K4s back. "We're assuming a record of where a person parked is fine to keep. True?" converts the whole example above into one sentence in week one.
  4. Only then descend — and descend on purpose. A spike is a permitted descent: time-boxed, aimed at one question, its code thrown away. Falling is a descent with no question and no time box. The difference is whether you can say, before you start, what you will know when you stop.

parking/ was built this way on purpose, and its history shows it: after the original demo arrived, the first commits were the requirements, the design, the policy, and a contract — documents and a layer-0 folder, before a single feature. The seven components that are still only READMEs are not behind schedule. They are waiting at the top of the waterfall until their requirement is settled.

2.7 Requirement, or design?

Students have trouble telling these apart, and the confusion is expensive in a specific way: a requirement cannot change without the client, and a design can. Put a design decision in the requirements and you have promised the client a table name. Put a requirement in the design and it can be quietly changed by whoever edits the file next.

Two questions separate them, and a sentence must pass both to be a requirement:

Could a different team satisfy this sentence a different way? Could you check it from outside, without reading the source?

Requirement (what must be observably true)Design (the mechanism we chose)
A stale count is never presented as current (U-2)The collector stamps receipt time; staleness is computed from age_ms
Every reading is attributable to a device (S-8)Ed25519 over an ordered, pipe-joined signing string
One command returns the system to a known state (T-5)docker compose down -v
Revocation takes effect no later than the next poll (I-4)The collector reads the devices table every cycle, so there is no cache to invalidate
Student identity is never joined to a locationsightings has no student_id column, and no view that could add one
A lot under maintenance reads as closed for service, not missing (E-9)A maintenance state distinct from down in the derived-state enum

The tells that a requirement has design in it: a table name, a library, a language, an HTTP status code, an endpoint path, an algorithm. The tells that a design has a requirement in it: "the user must be able to," with no mechanism following. And the rule from CONTRIBUTING.md: a pull request that edits a requirement to match what the code happens to do is a design change wearing a costume.

Keeping them apart is what makes the loop cheap. The design can be replaced in an iteration; the requirement is renegotiated with a person. DESIGN.md says it in its first paragraph: every mechanism there is replaceable without renegotiating a requirement.

2.8 From a user story to an API

The other thing students find hard is the middle: they can write a story and they can write an endpoint, and they cannot see how one turns into the other. The chain has four links, and the third is the one that gets skipped.

user story → acceptance criteria → acceptance test → observables the test needs → the contract
 who, what,    observable,            fails today       fields, states,               the API
 and why       numbered                                 endpoints

A story names a person, a want, and a reason: As a driver arriving for an event, I want to see which lots have space, so that I can go straight to one instead of circling. The reason is the part that matters; it is the pain, and the want is a guess about the solution.

Acceptance criteria turn it into observable sentences, each of which can be wrong: the page shows a count per lot; a count older than the staleness threshold is shown with its age and never as current; a lot whose data cannot be trusted reads as unknown, not missing. Those are U-1, U-2, and U-3 — a story became three numbered requirements.

The acceptance test is one of those sentences made executable, and it is written to fail today. In the style of T-2, driven through the sensor's control plane:

set lot 3's sensor to `stuck`          # 200 OK, frozen as_of
poll past the staleness threshold
GET the driver view
assert lot 3 shows a count with an age, and is not marked current

The observables are what the test had to reach for in order to assert anything. To assert "with an age," the driver view must expose an age. To assert "not marked current," it must expose a state. To drive the fault, the sensor must have a control plane. So the test forces fields into existence: age_ms, a derived live/stale/unreachable, POST /control/mode. Those fields are the contract — S-1, C-4, and the control table in REQUIREMENTS.md — and they exist because a test needed to observe them, not because someone thought they would be nice.

That is the whole trick. An API is the set of observables your acceptance tests need. Design the tests and the API falls out; design the API first and you will ship fields nobody can test and miss the ones somebody needed.

The VIP story from docs/TEAM-AND-CLIENT.md runs the same chain and shows it producing something harder. As a season-ticket holder, I want to hold a space for thirty minutes, so that I am not circling when I arrive. Criteria: a hold is visible to its holder with its expiry; the last space goes to exactly one of two simultaneous requests; a retried request does not take a second space. Tests: two concurrent holds, assert one wins; the same request twice, assert one hold. Observables forced: an expires_at, an idempotency key on the request, and a race decided in one database statement. The test for "exactly one wins" is what puts the decision inside the database rather than in whichever request the load balancer saw first — the design was chosen by the test.

Two consequences:

  • A story with no test is a wish. It cannot be wrong, so it cannot be finished.
  • A test with no story is accidental complexity. It protects something nobody asked for, and it will be maintained forever.

T-3 is this chain as a rule: every "must" has a test that names its ID. Grep the ID and you walk from the test back to the sentence, and from the sentence back to the person.

The scoreboard

The chain has a side effect that is worth more than it looks. When tests are named by the story or requirement they serve, and grouped that way in the output, a test run stops being a count of passes and becomes a board: these stories are green, those are still red, this one turned green today. That is gamification — progress shown as a score people want to move — in the only form that is honest, because every point on the board could have been lost. A team watches its own board turn green in groups that mean a driver can now do this. A client reads the same board without reading a line of code, and it is a fence they can check between demos. T-3 gives parking/ the board for free: run node --test and the names carry the IDs, so sorting the output by U-, C-, S- is sorting it by promise.

The board is only honest while the tests are. The moment a green can be produced without the behavior — a test that asserts nothing, a skipped suite that reports success — the score detaches from the thing it measured, and the game is being played against yourself.

2.10 What keeps humans and agents comfortable

The essay makes discomfort the leading indicator. The shape of the work is where you control it.

Humans are comfortable when the next step is small, visible, and reversible. Concretely:

  • They know what done means before they start, because it is a sentence in the queue.
  • They can see the fence — the demo is on the calendar, the deploy is routine — so the fear near a deadline has nothing to attach to.
  • Decisions are two-way doors wherever possible, so being wrong costs an afternoon.
  • There is slack in the plan, so K2 arriving is a planning event and not a crisis.
  • They can watch tests turn from red to green in groups that mean a story is satisfied — the scoreboard in section 2.7. Progress that is visible, honest, and theirs is the cheapest comfort there is, and the client draws the same comfort from the same board.
  • Bad news early is rewarded, visibly, every time, so it arrives early.
  • Nobody is blamed for K2. The retro says findable by X; X is now on the list, and does not say who should have asked.
  • The uncomfortable top-of-waterfall work is made concrete: a checklist of questions, a numbered document, a K4 to read aloud. Discomfort shrinks when the task has a shape.

Agents do not feel anything, and the analogue is exact: what keeps an agent reliable is the same list, made literal, because an agent arrives every session knowing nothing and leaves remembering nothing.

  • It needs the working set in the repository — REQUIREMENTS, DESIGN, QUEUE, FINDINGS — because there is no hallway in which to be told the constraint.
  • It needs a task small enough to review, with a test that defines done, because review capacity is the bottleneck and "this should work" is K3 by construction.
  • It needs the contract before the code, because it will otherwise invent one, fluently, and every session will invent a different one.
  • It needs the K4s written down, because a K4 held only in a head is a K2 to the agent and it will hand you a clean, tested violation.
  • It needs one command to run and one to reset, because a machine that cannot run the thing cannot check its own claim.

And one asymmetry that decides where the human stands. The bottom three steps of the waterfall are where an agent is fastest and most useful: designs, code, tests, fixtures, migrations, the second and third test case. The top three are where it is no help at all, because they are conversations with people, laws, and a client's sense of proportion. So the arrival of agents moves the human up the waterfall. The requirement document is the railing that keeps a machine-speed descent from becoming a machine-speed fall, and the "(test)" at each step is how the human stays in the loop without reading every line.

3.2 A test is a fear with a name

The ArduinoUnit guidebook opens with a hot dog cart: a cart that only rolls forward and left, a heater that takes thirty minutes to start, a chiller that leaks, a money box that opens four times in five. You will be out of business soon. Then the arithmetic. N independent parts at reliability P work together with probability P^N. Ten parts at 90% is 35% — not a bridge anyone wants to use. For ten parts to give 99%, each must be 99.9%.

Solving bigger problems reliably requires more reliable components.

A reliable thing works when in good repair and used correctly, and has detectable error status when it is broken or misused.

That second sentence is the whole subject, and it has two halves: it works, and it says so when it does not. Most testing effort goes to the first half. Most production pain comes from the second.

SOFTWARE-ENGINEERING.md says every practice retires a fear, and names tests as retiring "I don't know if I just broke something." That is true of a suite as a whole and too coarse to act on. Testing is a family, and each member retires a different fear. The guidebook's list of what to test is, read this way, a list of fears:

StatusIs a component broken right now?"it is broken and nobody can tell"/health (S-5); live, stale, unreachable (C-4)
InputIs the input absent, late, wrong, or lying?"garbage got in"C-1, C-5, C-7; the whole F switchboard
OutputIs the result in bounds and in the right shape?"we said something impossible"S-7; one error envelope (V-5)

Two rules from the essay apply with full force:

  • A test that reduces no one's anxiety will be dropped under pressure, and should be. If you cannot say which row a test belongs to, find the row or delete the test.
  • A test that reduces anxiety without reducing risk is worse than none. A test that cannot fail. A mock that agrees with everything. A suite that is green because it was skipped. Watch every test fail once — CONTRIBUTING.md requires it — because a green you have never seen red is a K3 with a checkmark on it.

The question you can ask

"Which fear does this test retire, and have I ever seen it fail?"

Three of these deserve more than a row.

The power-on self test. If your thing can't run without certain things, make sure those things are ok or just stop. The larger-system failure is the opposite: start anyway, log a warning, and serve wrong answers until someone reads the log. parking/ has one of each. db.js exits when DATABASE_URL is missing — a self test. index.js warns when JWT_SECRET is missing and continues with dev-secret — a self test that lets you drive off with the hood open. The second is a chosen falsehood for a demo, and it is the exact shape of every "temporary" default that reaches production. The rule: a missing precondition is an exit code, not a warning, unless someone has written down why not.

Sanity, repeated, with a safe state. The guidebook reruns a sanity test every 250 milliseconds and soft-resets the board when it fails, on the theory that a reboot might help. The general form is invariants that are checked while the system runs — not only in the suite — and a stated, tested move to a safe state when one is violated. Restart the container. Fail over. Serve the last good count with its age attached (E-7, U-1). The reboot is not the embarrassing part. The embarrassing part is a system that has noticed it is insane and keeps answering.

IO, and the chicken and the egg. You cannot test input handling without controlling the input, and the code that reads the real input is exactly the code you want to test. ArduinoUnit's answer is MockStream plus a design rule — serve, don't rule — which section 3.7 is about. parking/ gives the same answer at system scale: the sensor is a real service that can be told to be slow, down, stuck, wrong, flapping, or skewed (F), and the mock and the real thing answer to one conformance check (T-4), so swapping them is an event with a result rather than a discovery.

3.4 Repeatability is a property of state

A repeatable test gives the same verdict every run — alone, beside any other test, in any order, on any machine. The essay says why this matters: a flaky test trains a team to ignore red, and after that the suite that would have caught the real failure has been disarmed.

Repeatability is not a property of the test. It is a property of the state the test touches, and that state has exactly three scopes:

ScopeWhat it meansRepeatable?Examples
Localthe test creates it and destroys ityes, and safe to run in parallela MockStream declared inside the test; a pure function's arguments; a temporary directory; a fresh object
Shardedshared infrastructure, partitioned so each test or worker owns a sliceyes — if the key is unique and the test stays inside its sliceone database schema per worker; one lot id per test; one sensor container per job; a trace id
Globalone instance, read and written by everyoneno. A test that modifies global state is a race condition, not a test.a shared development database; a seeded row two tests update; environment variables; the system clock; a singleton; a fixed port

The third row is the one to memorize. A test that writes global state passes when run alone, then fails when another test runs first, or at the same time, or against the rows yesterday's run left behind. The failure is order-dependent, nondeterministic, and reproduces most reliably on the machine of the person least able to fix it. Everything you have ever heard about flaky tests is this row.

The fixes, in order of preference:

  1. Make the state local. Pull the logic out of the state (section 3.7) and pass the dependency in. The tests in parking/contract/ touch no state at all, which is why node --test can run every file in its own process at once and nothing needs a lock.
  2. Shard it. Give each worker its own slice, with a key it owns: a schema named for the worker, a lot the test creates and drops, a container per job. F-1 — each fault mode settable per sensor — is what makes sensors shardable. One sensor shared by two tests that both set its mode is a race.
  3. Serialize it, and say so. --test-concurrency=1, a lock, a mutex. This works and it does not scale. Write down why it was chosen so the next person can undo it.

Read-only global state is fine; a fixture nobody writes is repeatable. The dangerous combination is global and mutated.

Two pieces of global state hide in plain sight:

  • The clock. Date.now() is a global read that returns a different value every run. T-1 bans unseeded randomness for exactly the reason it should ban the wall clock: a test whose inputs change without a change to the code cannot fail the same way twice. Inject the clock. The pivot in docs/TEAM-AND-CLIENT.md shows what it costs to learn this late — a thirty-minute hold that expires by a clock no test can set.
  • The reset. T-5 — one command returns the whole system to a known state — is the global-state fallback made cheap. docker compose down -v is it. It is a confession that some state is global, and the honest response to that confession is to make it cost ten seconds.

The question you can ask

"What does this test write, and who else can read it?"

3.5 Repeatability is a property of the environment

Same test, same state, different machine. Now the question is whether the software around the test is the same.

Containers: repeatable, almost

A container reproduces one machine: the filesystem, the toolchain, every library at the version it was installed. parking/ runs in one — docker compose up --build — and T-5 is one command because of it.

Two things make "the container is the same everywhere" a K4 rather than a K0, and both are worth knowing exactly.

An image tag is a family, not a file. node:20-alpine on an M-series Mac pulls the arm64 variant; on a GitHub-hosted Ubuntu runner it pulls amd64. Same Dockerfile, two architectures, two builds of every package inside. They behave the same almost always. Not always: native modules are compiled per architecture; some packages have no arm64 build and fall back to emulation, which is slow and occasionally wrong; numerical libraries differ — Intel's MKL is x86-only, so even a CPU build of a numerical package is a different artifact on arm64; and anything timing-sensitive runs at a different speed. A container makes the software identical and the machine similar. There are three honest choices: pin --platform linux/amd64 and run under emulation on Apple silicon, which is slow and makes the developer's machine the odd one out; build for both and test on both, which GitHub's arm64 runners now make ordinary; or accept almost and write down where it is load-bearing. Any of the three is fine. Not knowing which one you chose is not.

An image is only as repeatable as its inputs. node:20-alpine floats to the newest 20.x at pull time. npm install resolves fresh at build time unless a lock file is present and used. At this writing parking/app/ has no package-lock.json, and app/Dockerfile copies only package.json before running npm install — so two builds a week apart can contain different versions of express, jsonwebtoken, and pg, and nothing in the repository records which. The container is repeatable at the level of the operating system and not at the level of the code. That is a finding, not an accusation, and section 3.5 is the fix.

Meta package managers: repeat the decision, not the machine

conda and pixi take the other approach. Describe the environment as constraints — python >=3.12,<3.14, pytorch >=2.13,<3 — and let a resolver produce a lock that records one exact resolution per platform. A container reproduces one machine. A lock reproduces one decision procedure, run separately on every machine.

That is the right tool whenever something genuinely varies across the machines your users have. The ml/ demo in the languages course repository is the worked example, and its table of when a container is the wrong tool is worth repeating:

ConditionWhy a container failsExample
dependencies differ by hardwarean image pins one answer; GPU passthrough is Linux and NVIDIA onlyCUDA vs. Metal vs. CPU

Stay with a container when the build is hermetic, hardware-independent, and the target is Linux anyway. Both tools answer the same fear — "it works on my machine" — and reproduce different things. If nothing varies across your users' machines, the container gives the stronger guarantee.

3.6 What to pin: the major and the minor, not the revision

The position, stated plainly: in the manifest, say what you are compatible with. In the lock, record what you ran. Commit both.

manifest — a promise about compatibility      lock — a record of one resolution
express    ^4.19        (>=4.19, <5)           express  4.19.2   sha512-…
pytorch    >=2.13,<3                           pytorch  2.13.0   cpu_mkl_py312_h999dc20_102

Semantic versioning is the interface: the major changes when something breaks, the minor when something is added, the revision when something is fixed. That is V-4 in REQUIREMENTS.md, applied by strangers to their own libraries. So the manifest names the major — the promise you depend on — and the minor — the floor of features you actually use — and leaves the revision to the resolver. The lock pins the revision, and the hash, so that everyone who installs gets the same bytes and continuous integration resolves exactly what you tested.

Why this and not one of the alternatives:

Policy in the manifestRepeatable?When a CVE lands in a dependencyWhat actually happens

The difference between the last two rows is the difference between eliminating most security problems in an hour and, commonly, never. Both rows are repeatable. Only one of them survives.

That is the balance the policy strikes: repeatability against survivability. A fully pinned manifest is maximally repeatable and dies of neglect. An unpinned one is maximally current and never the same twice. Major.minor plus a lock is the point on the curve where both are affordable. It is not free, because semantic versioning is a promise made by strangers and revisions do sometimes break things, and two practices are what keep the promise honest:

  • The update is followed by the suite. The test suite is what makes taking a fix an hour instead of a project. This is the sentence that ties the document together: the quality of your tests sets the price of every security fix you will ever take. A team with a suite it trusts merges the dependency update the day it appears. A team without one leaves the pull request open for a year, which is the "never" row wearing a different costume.
  • The update is scheduled. Like deletion, it earns no credit and so it has to be somebody's named job, or a bot's — Dependabot, Renovate, a Monday task. A lock file that is never regenerated is a revision pin with extra steps.

The same policy applies one level up. node:20-alpine pins the major of the runtime, and docker compose pull followed by a rebuild is the update. The languages course's Rust demo floats its toolchain on stable and says in its README that a new release can turn the badge red without a change in the repository — the same trade-off, made the other way, with the reason written down. What is not acceptable is the unwritten version: a machine that works because of what happens to be installed on it.

For parking/, the fix for the finding in section 3.4 is three lines. Generate app/package-lock.json and commit it. Copy it into the image beside package.json. Replace npm install --omit=dev with npm ci --omit=dev, which refuses to run when the lock and the manifest disagree. That is the whole distance from almost repeatable to repeatable, and survivable.

3.7 Testing what runs somewhere else

The idiot light for a repository

Continuous integration is the idiot light with a URL, and the badge on a README is pin 13. It retires two fears — "it works on my machine" and "we only ever built it on one kind of machine" — and two rules make it worth trusting:

  • CI runs the same command you do. If the workflow's build step is ./build.sh and so is yours, there is no second recipe to drift. CONTRIBUTING.md names the three checks CI runs for parking/ — signed commits, parkctl conform --all --json, and the integration compose — and all three are commands a person runs by hand. None of them is in a workflow file yet. By the repository's own rule that is work to be done, not an error in the document.
  • The gate is in the script, not only in the workflow. A build script that runs the tests and stops on failure cannot produce artifacts locally that CI would have rejected.

Platforms: build there, and run there

Compiling for a platform and running on it are different tests, and a cross-compiler can only make the first claim. The languages course's C++ demo builds and runs the same program on six legs — Linux, macOS, and Windows, each on x86_64 and arm64, each on a runner whose processor really is that architecture — with fail-fast: false, because when it breaks the interesting question is always which platforms broke, and stopping at the first one hides that.

The matrix is the container nuance from section 3.4 taken seriously: if you ship to two architectures, test on two.

Two honest caveats, from that repository's own notes. A toolchain that floats on stable can turn the badge red without a change. And a path-filtered workflow never starts for commits outside its path, which reads as pending forever if it is ever made a required check.

User interfaces

UI tests fail for one reason more than any other: they are global-state tests in disguise. A shared server, a shared browser, the real clock, and animations that take a variable number of milliseconds. Every fix in section 3.3 applies — one server per job, one browser context per test, injected time — and one fix from section 3.7 applies first: pull the logic out of the UI. app/public/app.js never computes available; the server derives it in one SQL fragment, and that is why the number can be tested without a browser. The translation lookup t(key, params) is a pure function of a key and a table; test it as one.

Then three loops, cheapest first. Push each class of error down to the cheapest loop that can catch it:

Assert on text and state, not on pixels. A screenshot comparison fails when a font is hinted differently on arm64, which is the container nuance arriving through a side door.

Deployments

A deployment is a test whose failure is an outage. The pattern is already in REQUIREMENTS.md, written for firmware and true everywhere: a deploy is not finished when the bits land; it is finished when the thing reports healthy, and the system should be the one to notice (G-5). Health-gated, with automatic revert, with the rollback timed before the window opens (E-8).

In GitHub Actions terms: a staging environment brought up from the same compose file; /health polled with a timeout as the power-on self test; the integration suite run against it; promotion only on green. The deploy script is the one you run by hand. And the maintenance window is a state, not a fault (E-9) — a deploy that turns the dashboard red every night teaches the operators to ignore red.

3.8 Serve, don't rule

The guidebook's last argument is the one that changes how code gets written. Tests observe code, and measuring something changes it — but the useful change is not in the code under observation. It is in the habits of the person writing it, who starts to write code that is easier to observe.

The example is a temperature. One version marches from the analog pin to Fahrenheit in a single function that is wrong somewhere, with no way to find out where. The other separates the rule — the two lines that touch the pin and the global — from the serve — three pure conversions, each testable against a number you can look up. A good compiler emits the same code for both.

parking/ is built on this rule at every layer:

  • The signing string is a pure function of a reading (contract/signing.js), so S-8 has a test that runs in a millisecond and needs no key, no sensor, and no network.
  • available is derived in one SQL fragment, not stored, so it can be wrong in one place instead of five.
  • Skew is arithmetic — (receipt − as_of) − age_ms — computed from three numbers a test can choose.
  • The collector is a library, not a service, so it can be called in-process with a fetch function that lies.
  • contract/ holds no state at all, and every one of its tests is free.

The ratio of serve to rule is the testability of a design, and the diagnostic runs backwards: if something is hard to test, that is information about the design, not about testing.

3.9 Three lessons the guidebook ends with

Slow and steady wins the race. Solve problems in parts you can test; test the parts together; keep the parts and their tests when you are done, because the hot dog cart is going to become a taco stand. Everybody's does. In this repository the taco stand has a name — the VIP pivot in docs/TEAM-AND-CLIENT.md — and what survives it is exactly the code that was built in testable parts.

A journey of a thousand miles begins with a map. Planning every detail is a waste; so is charging into the woods. The map is the tests: if you do not know how to make a thing work, write the tests that would pass when it does, and they will tell you what the thing is — including what it does not have to be. T-3 is this as a rule: every "must" has a test that names it. F-6 is this for adversaries: demonstrate the attack before building the defense, because a defense against an attack nobody has performed is a K3 with a code review.

Your momma writes better tests than you. You cannot see past your own design. The only things that find K3 are reality and a person who does not share the assumption — a reviewer, the client at a demo, the attacker in F-6. Have other people say what needs testing. Take their advice.

3.10 Checklists

Before trusting a test suite

  • Every test owns its state, or owns a unique shard of it.
  • The suite passes in any order and in parallel. Try it; node --test already runs files in parallel.
  • No wall clock, no unseeded randomness, no fixed port.
  • Every test has been seen red.
  • CI runs the command you run, on every platform you ship to.

Before trusting an environment

  • The manifest names the major and the minor; the lock names the revision; both are committed.
  • The install refuses to run when the lock and the manifest disagree: npm ci, cargo --locked, pixi install --locked.
  • The base image pins its major; the architectures you ship to are written down and tested.
  • The update is somebody's named job, and the suite runs after it.
  • One command returns everything to a known state.

Before trusting a deploy

  • A power-on self test: a missing precondition is an exit code.
  • A health check with a timeout, and an automatic revert when it is not met.
  • The rollback has been run, and timed.
  • The deploy script is the one you run by hand.

4 Six Ideas Underneath the Practice

Six ideas underneath the practice: where the problem comes from, what software is made of, and why it costs what it costs.

SOFTWARE-ENGINEERING.md argues that most of engineering is managing what you don't know and what you're afraid of. This document is about the other half — the material you're working in and the problem you're working on — and it assumes you have read nothing. Not that file, not Brooks, not a textbook. Where a term is needed it is defined here.

4.1 Terms used here

Five categories of not-knowing, because they come up throughout. (SOFTWARE-ENGINEERING.md makes the full argument; this is enough to read on.)

  • Contract — the promises one part of a system makes to another, written down: what it accepts, what it returns, and how it fails. Not the code that keeps them.

4.2 Nobody hands you the problem

You are handed a sentence. "We need an app that shows which lots have spaces."

That sentence is not a requirement. It is a wish with a user interface already attached, and it is the most dangerous input you will receive on the entire project.

"Gathering requirements" is a false metaphor

The phrase suggests requirements exist — out there, in the client's head — and your job is to collect them, like mushrooms. Nothing exists yet except two things:

  • a pain, which is real evidence, and
  • a proposed solution, which is a guess.

Clients almost always know their domain far better than you do. That is exactly what makes their proposed solution dangerous: it arrives pre-formed, confident, and backed by the authority of the person paying. It does not look like a question, so it triggers no investigation. That is the precise shape of K3 — certainty that costs nothing at the time and everything later — except it is now someone else's certainty, which you have inherited without an audit.

The move is to walk back up from the solution to the pain. What happens today when a driver can't find a space? Who gets the phone call? What do they say? This question is nearly free, takes one minute, and routinely changes the product. In the parking case the answer might be "the gate staff radio each other and guess" — which means the real customer might be gate staff, not drivers, and the real requirement might be about trust in a number rather than about a map.

Keep the pain. Test the solution.

Writing it down is how you find out what they want

The second surprise is that requirements do not exist even in principle until someone writes a candidate down. The client cannot tell you what should happen when a sensor goes silent, because they have never owned a sensor and have never thought about it. They can absolutely tell you — in about two seconds — when they read your sentence saying the count freezes at its last known value and the page keeps showing it as current. "No! Absolutely not."

So the first draft's job is to be wrong in a way that is easy to correct. This has a consequence students consistently get backwards:

Specific and wrong beats vague and safe.

"The page refreshes every five seconds" gets corrected. "The page is responsive and up to date" gets nodded at, agreed to, and means nothing — and you will discover in week nine that it meant something different to each of you. Vagueness feels safer because nobody can object to it. That is the problem: you have written a sentence that cannot be wrong, so it cannot teach you anything.

This is the same move as a test that fails before you fix the code. You state the precise claim so that reality is able to reject it.

The questions that actually extract requirements

None of these are about features. All of them get answers nobody volunteers.

AskBecause
"How many? And in two years?""A city" is a mood. A number with a horizon is a design input.
"What are we explicitly not building?"The cheapest scope decision there is, and it ends the same argument permanently.
"What happens if this input is absent? late? wrong? lying?"Four cases every consumer inherits by accident if nobody asks.
"What does a bad day look like?"Surfaces availability and recovery, which nobody thinks to state.
"How long may it be down, and when?"Unstated, the answer is 100%, the most expensive requirement you can accidentally accept.
"What would you do differently with this answer?"Kills features fast. If nothing changes, nobody needs it.
"Who is affected who isn't in this room?"Bystanders, the surveilled, the person whose lot shows the wrong number.

And one rule about all of them: a requirement with no price attached is a wish. Every sentence in a requirements document is a purchase that somebody has to authorize.

In parking/

REQUIREMENTS.md is what a constructed requirement set looks like. Three things in it are worth studying as technique, not content:

The E section is arithmetic done on the client's answers, not a transcript of them. The client said a hundred lots, a hundred thousand spaces, an event crowd arriving over two hours. Nobody said anything about staleness thresholds. But E-2 turns the crowd into 1.7 vehicles per second into a single filling lot, and E-5 then observes that a count ten seconds old is already wrong by seventeen spaces — so the staleness threshold in N-3 and U-2 is derived from the fill rate, rather than chosen as a round number. Nobody could have handed you that requirement. It had to be built out of two things the client did know.

E-6 shows that the shape of an answer matters as much as its value. Availability is stated as five seconds a day outside 02:00–04:00 and one hour inside. The section then points out that the same system is 99.994% available if you quote the day it protects and 95.8% if you quote the whole day — which is why the window has to be named rather than folded into a number of nines. A single number would have been easier to agree to and would have meant nothing.

E-10 is a requirement no client would ever state. Local time is not monotonic: twice a year an hour never happens and an hour happens twice, so the maintenance window is sometimes one hour wide instead of two and an instant inside the repeated hour is ambiguous. That started as K2 — nobody knew to ask — and somebody moved it to K0 by thinking about the edge before it arrived at 2am on a Sunday in March.

Then notice what the document does with what it doesn't have. Under E there is a heading called "Not yet answered", listing four open recovery questions, and it says plainly: until these are answered these are guesses nobody has agreed to. That is the honest state of a real requirements document. Compare it to the Scope decisions and Non-goals sections, which record what was decided against, so it stops being re-argued.

On your project

Your client is probably an instructor, a stand-in stakeholder, or your own imagination. The failure mode of an imagined client is that you become your own client and are never corrected — every guess you make is confirmed by the only person who could dispute it.

So: nominate an actual human, and go get corrected by them on a schedule you set in advance. Where there is genuinely nobody, write requirements in a form that can still be wrong — numbered, observable, testable — and put the numbers in even when you had to invent them, marked as invented. An invented number that is written down can be challenged. An assumed one cannot.

How this fails

You build exactly what was asked, on time, correctly, and it is not used. No test suite in existence detects this. Only a person looking at the running thing does — which is why a demo to a real human, early and repeatedly, is not a courtesy. It is the only instrument you have for this entire class of error.

4.3 State is the material

Every discipline has a material, and its material's properties explain most of its rules. Concrete is strong in compression and weak in tension, so you put steel in the tension side and every rule about rebar follows. Software's material is not code. Code is the description. The thing that actually runs is state, plus the rules that change it.

Why state, specifically, is the hard part

A function that takes inputs and returns a value is easy in a way you may not have noticed. You can call it. You can call it again and get the same answer. You can test it by writing down an input and an expected output, and that test will be true forever.

State has four properties, and each one takes something away from you.

It persists. It outlives the code that created it. So when you change the code, the new code meets data written by the old code — data you cannot reach and cannot re-run. That is the whole migration problem (V-7). The smallest possible demonstration is in this repository: db/init.sql runs only on an empty volume, so if you change the schema and run docker compose up without down -v, you get new code and the old database, no error message, and behavior nobody designed.

It is shared. Two parties read the same value, and they can disagree about what it is right now.

It ages. A value that was true when you read it may be false when you use it. Freshness becomes a property of data that the type system knows nothing about.

It changes while being observed. Two writers, one value, no agreed order.

From which comes the definition worth memorizing:

The difficulty of a system is not how many features it has. It is how much state a change can reach.

That is also what "coupling" actually means, which otherwise stays a vague virtue you get marked down for violating.

Anything that might be out of date is a cache

This is the single most useful renaming you can perform on your own code. A cache is not a thing you install; it is any copy of state that might not match the original. Once you call something a cache, you inherit the complete list of questions about it: how stale can it get, how would you know, and what happens to the user while it is wrong?

Go and find every cache in your system that nobody labelled one. A variable holding something fetched five minutes ago. An array in the browser that a setInterval refreshes. A denormalized count column. A JSON Web Token.

parking/ contains the same design decision made twice, in opposite directions, which is why it is a good place to learn this.

The app's login token is a cache. app/index.js signs a token carrying role, lot_id, and is_root, with expiresIn: '8h'. getUserFromRequest verifies the signature and returns the payload — it never re-reads the user row. So if an admin demotes someone, that person keeps their old permissions for up to eight hours. Described as authentication, that sounds like a quirk. Described accurately — a cache with an eight-hour TTL and no invalidation — it tells you exactly what can go wrong and how bad it can be.

The device registry is deliberately not a cache. DESIGN.md has the collector read the devices table on every poll cycle, and says why: it makes revocation effective within one interval (I-4) "without a cache to invalidate." Same system, same kind of decision, opposite answer — because revoking a compromised sensor is not something you can afford to have take effect eight hours later.

Neither choice is universally right. Being able to say which one you made, and what it costs, is the skill.

There is no single "now"

Once state lives in two places joined by a network, there is no shared present tense. In parking/: the sensor knows a count; the collector knows what the sensor said four seconds ago; the browser knows what the app said three seconds before that. All three are locally correct and they disagree.

So the design question is never "keep them in sync," which is not achievable. It is:

How wrong may each copy be, and who gets told?

That question is the entire C and U sections of the requirements. And note E-5: at peak, a count that is fresh by the clock — ten seconds old — is already off by seventeen spaces. Freshness and correctness are different properties, and a system can fail the second while passing the first.

Four beliefs that feel like K0 and are K3, in the order they cost the most:

The beliefReality
The message arrivesIt may not. (C-1: every call gets an explicit timeout.)
It arrives onceA retry means it may arrive twice.
It arrives in orderTwo paths, two latencies, no guarantee.
The clock is rightN, and the skewed fault, which produces plausible data with a valid signature and a clock off by minutes.
The write succeeded because the call returnedThe response may be what got lost.

The defense against arriving twice is worth naming because it is a design technique rather than a warning: make the operation idempotent — doing it twice equals doing it once — and "did that go through?" stops being a question you have to answer. "Set available to 37" is idempotent. "One car arrived" is not. That is not an abstract example; it is the difference between the reading model in S-1 and the old push endpoint POST /api/lots/:id/occupancy that REQUIREMENTS.md marks for retirement.

Time deserves its own paragraph

parking/ splits one apparently simple idea — how old is this? — into two fields, and the reason is the best small lesson about state in the repository.

  • as_of (S-2) is the wall-clock time the count was taken. It is meaningful across machines and it is a lie whenever the clock is wrong.
  • age_ms (S-3) is elapsed time from a monotonic source — a counter that only ever moves forward and does not jump when the clock is corrected. It is meaningless across machines and it cannot be forged by a bad clock.

Staleness is computed only from age_ms (N-3), so a sensor with a fast clock cannot make old data look fresh. Then N-4 has the collector stamp its own receipt time, because two independent clocks are what make disagreement detectable at all — and N-5 says that when age_ms and as_of disagree, the conclusion is the clock is broken, not the data is old.

Wall clock and monotonic clock are different instruments measuring different things. Almost every time-related bug you will write for the next five years is using one where the other was required.

Practical moves

  • Derive instead of store. Anything computed can't go stale. app/index.js computes available in SQL as CASE WHEN is_open THEN capacity - occupancy ELSE 0 END, in one shared fragment reused by every lot query, and the browser never computes it. One place can be wrong instead of five.
  • Say which copy is authoritative. S-6: the sensor reports its capacity, the database remains authoritative, and a mismatch is a fault, not a correction. Any value that exists twice needs this sentence written down somewhere.
  • Put state's age in the data, not just the label. age_ms is a field, so it can be tested; "last updated" in the corner of a page cannot.
  • Push logic away from state. Pure functions in the middle, state at the edges. contract/ is layer 0 and holds no state at all, which is exactly why its eighteen tests are free to run.
  • Record what a decision was based on. C-9 requires the inputs to every derived state: "Marked lot 3 stale: age 47s exceeded threshold 15s", not "sensor returned 200." State that explains itself is state you can debug a week later.

On your project

Make a list of every piece of state in your system and, for each: who owns it, who else has a copy, how stale a copy may be, and what happens to a user while it is wrong. It takes twenty minutes. It is the highest-value twenty minutes available to you, and roughly none of it is visible in a class diagram.

Then notice which of your components hold no state at all. Those are the ones that will be easy for the rest of the term, and that is not a coincidence.

4.4 Essential and accidental complexity

Some of the difficulty in your project comes from the problem. The rest comes from how you chose to build it. Fred Brooks named these in 1986 — essential and accidental complexity — and the distinction is the only reliable way to end a design argument.

Essential complexity is in the problem. It survives every rewrite, every framework, every language, and every model. parking/ has a lot of it, and all of it is traceable to a sentence somebody agreed to:

  • Sensors lie, freeze, hang, and drift (F) — because real hardware does.
  • Clocks disagree (N) — because they do.
  • The network drops requests (C) — because it does.
  • A driver must be told the difference between a live count, an old count, and no data (U-1) — because showing "12 spaces" for a forty-minute-old reading is the failure the system exists to prevent.
  • Sensors cannot be upgraded all at once, so mixed versions are the normal condition (V-3).
  • Parking data is location data about people, so "counts, not locations" is a scope decision with an ethical reason.
  • Eight languages, because the users have eight languages.

You cannot engineer any of that away. You can only decide where to handle it.

Accidental complexity is yours. The build step, the bundler, the ORM, the five layers of indirection, the config format you invented on a Tuesday. All real work, all needing maintenance, none of it demanded by anyone.

The question that settles it

What requirement would have to disappear for this complexity to go away?

If you can name one, it is essential — and the conversation is with the client about whether that requirement is worth its price, not with your teammate about code style. If the honest answer is "none," it is accidental, it is yours, and it has to justify its carrying cost like anything else.

This matters more than it sounds, because most design arguments are category disputes conducted as taste disputes. One person says "this is too complicated"; the other says "the problem is complicated." Both are true. Neither is responsive. The question above converts an argument with no end into a question with an answer.

The trap

Accidental complexity you introduced is invisible to you, because you understand it. You built it, so it costs you nothing to navigate, and you will sincerely believe it is simple. It is charged to strangers — and in about a month, to you.

This is why "it's simple once you know how it works" is not a defense. Everything is.

In parking/

Look at what this repository refuses, and at the reason attached to each refusal:

None of those are minimalism for its own sake. Each is a decision that the essential complexity of this system — lying sensors, skewed clocks, provenance — is hard enough that no attention should be spent anywhere else. Every dependency removed is a thing a student does not have to learn, trust, audit, or update.

And the last two are worth studying as a technique: when you meet accidental complexity, first try to make it not exist. Canonical JSON is a genuinely hard problem, correctly solved by nobody in this repository, because the design sidesteps the need for it.

The part about agents

Brooks's actual claim in 1986 was that most of the easy accidental complexity had already been harvested, so no single tool would ever deliver an order-of-magnitude improvement. Coding agents are the first serious test of that claim in forty years, and the result so far fits it exactly.

Agents are extremely good at accidental complexity: boilerplate, glue, translation packs, the second and third test case, the fixture, the migration script. They are no help at all with essential complexity, because essential complexity is what the problem is, and deciding what the problem is was never the typing.

So the enormous speedup people report is real, and it is concentrated in the part that was never the hard part. That is not a disappointment; it is the shape of the thing, and it tells you where to spend what you saved.

On your project

Write your system's essential complexity on one page. Five to fifteen lines, each traceable to a requirement. Anything you are maintaining that does not appear on that page is accidental, and should be able to say why it exists.

Do it in week three, when the answer is still short.

4.5 Conceptual integrity

It is better to ship one coherent design missing several good ideas than a system containing every good idea and no coherence.

That is Brooks's central claim, and it is the least intuitive idea in this document, because every individual decision that destroys coherence was a good decision when it was made.

Why coherence beats correctness-per-part

People understand systems by analogy. You learn how one part works, and then you guess how the next part works. Coherence is what makes the guess right.

Every place your guess fails is something you must instead memorize. A person can memorize a few dozen exceptions before they stop trying — and what they do next is the thing that actually kills codebases: they stop reasoning and start copying whatever nearby code seems to work.

So a codebase does not become unmaintainable when it gets large. It becomes unmaintainable when guessing stops working. That can happen at four thousand lines.

It shows up first in vocabulary

The words are the visible surface of the design, and they are the cheapest thing to get right.

One thing, one word. If the client says "lot," the schema says "facility," and the UI says "garage," you do not have one system with three labels. You have three mental models and a translation layer that exists only inside one person's head — and that person is going to graduate.

One word, one thing. The reverse error is worse because it is silent. If "reading" means both the message a sensor sent and the row you stored, you will eventually confuse them somewhere the types cannot catch.

parking/ treats this as a requirement rather than a style preference. I-1 insists that four identities stay distinct — the device (which sensor), the caller (admin, ops, collector), the release key (who signed a firmware package), and the artifact (which firmware) — and gives the reason: an admin's login must never be the thing that signs a release. That is vocabulary discipline with a security consequence.

The sharpest example is a word the system deliberately declines to reuse. S-4 says status is the sensor's assessment of itself — ok or degraded — and that a sensor may never report that it is offline, because "if it can answer, it is not offline." Reachability is a conclusion the consumer draws. So the collector's derived state in C-4 — live, stale, unreachable — is a different concept, with a different name, computed by a different party. Two ideas that a careless design would have merged into one field called status, and every consumer downstream would have inherited the confusion.

It shows up second in shape

Once vocabulary is settled, uniformity of form does the same work:

  • One error envelope on every endpoint and every version (V-5), with code from a closed set and message never parsed by anything.
  • Retryability stated explicitly in the body (V-6) rather than inferred from the status code — "because the inference rules are exactly what people get wrong."
  • One README per component, all the same shape, each stating its contract.
  • Stable requirement IDs referenced from tests, reviews, and commits, so grep S-8 walks from a test to the sentence that demanded it.

Uniformity is not aesthetic. If everything is uniform, the one weird thing is a signal. If nothing is uniform, the weird thing is invisible.

And coherence is not rigidity: /health is deliberately not versioned, because a versioned discovery endpoint has a bootstrap problem — you would need to know the version in order to ask which versions exist. The exception is fine. The exception being written down, with its reason, is what keeps it from being noise.

Somebody has to hold it

Coherence is the one property that does not live in any part. It lives between the parts, which means no amount of careful local work produces it and no test can check it. Somebody has to be holding the whole shape in their head, and most of that person's job is saying no — including to good ideas, especially to good ideas, because a good idea is exactly what a coherent design most often has to refuse.

On a team of four this is an assignable role, not a title. Rotate it if you like. Leave it vacant and you will get four locally excellent designs and one incoherent system.

This is why AGENTS.md says humans own the structure and agents author and advise. An agent produces a locally reasonable design every session and remembers no conventions between them. Ten locally reasonable designs compose into something nobody can hold. The thing that cannot be delegated is coherence — not because agents write bad code, but because coherence is a property of the whole and each session only ever sees a part.

On your project

Keep a glossary. Ten to thirty terms, one line each, at the top of your requirements document. Treat the appearance of a new noun as an event that needs a decision: is this a new thing, or is it a second name for a thing we have?

It is the cheapest document you will ever write, and it is the one that makes everything else agree.

4.6 Locality of reasoning

Most courses justify interfaces with reuse: write it once, use it twice. That reason is weak. Reuse is a bonus when it happens, and a trap when you design for it — code shaped to serve two callers is usually worse at both jobs than two clear versions would have been.

The real reason is arithmetic about human memory.

Nobody can hold a large system in their head

So correctness has to be establishable in pieces. If you can check that A honors its contract, and separately check that B honors its contract, then you can use A and B together without re-reading A. Your confidence in the whole is assembled from confidence in the parts.

That property — call it locality of reasoning — is not a nicety. It is the only reason systems larger than one person's head can exist at all. Every rule you have been taught about modules, layers, encapsulation, and dependency direction is downstream of it.

The test of an abstraction

Can you use it correctly without reading its implementation?

If yes, it is an abstraction and it is doing its job. If you have to look inside to use it right, it is not an abstraction — it is a function call with extra steps, and the boundary it advertises is a lie. That is worse than no boundary at all, because a reader who trusts it will be wrong in a way they had no reason to check.

Abstractions fail the same way every time

Something true on the inside escapes to the outside. Four flavors, all present in parking/:

LeakWhat it looks like
FailureA call whose contract says "returns a number" and whose reality includes a timeout. C-1 exists because of this.
TimeSomething that looks like reading a variable and is a network round-trip.
MeaningF-2: the stuck fault returns HTTP 200. The abstraction says "200 means success"; success does not mean current, and a consumer that conflated them is now confidently wrong.
TrustF-4: the skewed fault — plausible data, valid signature, clock off by minutes. Everything verifies and the conclusion is still wrong.

From which the rule: a contract that does not state how it fails has not stated anything. This is why "absent, late, wrong, lying" is a requirements-level question and not an implementation detail. Failure behavior is part of the interface, or the interface is fiction.

What this justifies in parking/

Almost every structural rule in the repository is this idea wearing different clothes:

  • The dependency rule. cli/check-layering.sh enforces that a folder may require only from a strictly lower layer — contract at 0, libraries at 1, services at 2, and nothing may require a service because there is no layer 3. It exits nonzero so CI can gate on it, and the script says why in its own header: "without one, folder boundaries become decoration within a month." The rule is what lets you read layer 1 without reading layer 2.
  • A contract folder that exists before its consumers. contract/README.md states the purpose in one sentence: "It exists so that two people can build the sensor and the collector without talking to each other."
  • T-4, which is locality written as a requirement. A sensor written by anyone, in any language, can be verified against this specification without reading its source. That is the abstraction test, promoted to something CI can run.
  • I-5, which is locality in the data model. Any stored reading can be verified against the device registry without contacting the sensor — so a reading remains checkable years later, by someone who has never met the machine that produced it.
  • Choosing a library over a service on purpose. identity/ stays a library because verification must work without a network hop; making it a service would add a timeout and an outage that stops everything.

The same thing is true of people

The interface between two developers is the same object as the interface between two modules.

"I'll build the collector, you build the journal" only works if the contract between them is written down first. Otherwise you have not divided the work — you have scheduled an argument for week nine, and it will be resolved by whoever has less time to be flexible.

This is also the mechanism behind the most-quoted line in software management, Brooks's adding people to a late project makes it later: more people means many more pairs of people, every pair needs an interface, and the interfaces you did not write down get negotiated in meetings instead.

On your project

Before you split work between people, write the contract between the pieces — the function signatures, the data shapes, the error cases — and check it in. Then both people can start on the same day. It takes an hour and it is the difference between integrating continuously and integrating in a panic.

4.7 Software is capital you carry

Ask a student what a feature costs and they will tell you how long it takes to build. That is the down payment.

cost = build + (carry × lifetime)

Carry is everything you pay for as long as the code exists: attention (every future reader must route around it), review time, dependency upgrades, security surface, onboarding, and the chance it breaks something else when you change a neighbor. None of it appears on a schedule. All of it is real, and the sum of it is where most of the money in this industry goes.

Software is not inventory you ship and are done with. It is capital you carry.

What follows

  • "Should we build this?" was never a build-cost question. A feature that takes an afternoon and lives five years is not an afternoon's decision.
  • Every dependency is a permanent obligation — something someone must learn, trust, audit, and update, forever, including the week it publishes a CVE. Adding one is a proposal, not a decision.
  • Deletion is usually the highest-return work available, and nobody schedules it. Removing a feature nobody uses returns its carry every week for the rest of the project. It also earns no credit, which is exactly why it has to be a named, scheduled activity rather than a virtue you hope somebody has.
  • Sunk cost is sunk. Code you wrote is not an asset you must defend. The hours are gone whether you keep it or delete it; the only live question is what it costs from here.
  • The reader you are writing for is you, having forgotten. Most carry is paid by the future version of the person who wrote it, which is the one piece of good news in this section: you are optimizing for someone whose interests you can predict exactly.

Carry that you chose is fine

The point is not to carry nothing. It is that carry should be declared.

attic/parking/ is the original demo, kept deliberately, with the reason written down: it is the before-picture — competent code that satisfies almost none of these requirements, and none of what it is missing would be caught by a conventional review. That is carry with a purpose, stated, and nothing depends on it.

DESIGN.md ends with a section titled Deferred, whose first line is "Named so they are not mistaken for oversights" — signing key rotation, mTLS, a log pipeline. That is the opposite of carry: work explicitly not taken on, so that its absence is a decision instead of a gap.

And REQUIREMENTS.md schedules a deletion. The Migration note says the old push endpoint and the sensor role are superseded by device identity, and to retire both when the collector lands, because "two ingestion paths with no clear story is worse than either one alone." Deletion, written down in advance, with the reason.

Data is carried too, and it is the heaviest thing you own

Code you can rewrite in an afternoon. Data you cannot.

E-4 sizes the reading stream at twenty a second — 1.7 million rows a day, 630 million a year. Then E-8 draws the consequence, and it is the most sobering sentence in the requirements: an hour is not long against those row counts, so "a migration that scans the reading table is not a migration this system can perform."

Read that again. The data they will accumulate has removed a future option. Not made it expensive — removed it, inside a stated maintenance window they agreed to. That is what carry does when nobody counts it, and it is why persistent state is the part of a system with real mass.

The logging requirements make the same trade explicitly rather than by accident: L-12 notes that four sensors at a five-second interval already produce seventy thousand events a day, nearly all uneventful, so steady-state successes are recorded at debug severity or sampled while state transitions are always kept. Retention (A-5) is a policy executed out of band. Somebody decided what to carry.

On your project

At the end of every iteration, ask one question: what can we delete? Code, features, dependencies, config options, dead branches, a table nobody reads.

And keep the list of what you decided not to build. It is the cheapest documentation in any project: it ends the same argument three separate times, and it tells the version of you that exists in November why the obvious thing was not done in September.

4.8 Two laws worth memorizing

Goodhart's law

When a measure becomes a target, it stops measuring.

Not because people are dishonest. Structurally: the measure was a proxy for something you actually cared about, and optimizing the proxy detaches it from the thing.

  • Aim at test coverage percentage, and you get tests with no assertions.
  • Aim at velocity, and estimates inflate.
  • Aim at uptime, and incidents get reclassified as maintenance.
  • Aim at lines of code, and you get lines of code.

This document should be honest about the version that will affect you first: T-3 says every requirement that says "must" has a test naming its ID. The moment that is graded, it is possible to produce tests that name IDs and assert nothing at all. That satisfies the letter of the rule and destroys the entire reason for it, and it will look like compliance in every report anybody runs.

The only defense is to keep the reason attached to the rule everywhere the rule appears. A practice whose purpose has been forgotten is already being gamed, including by people who think they are following it.

parking/ has two requirements that exist purely to protect a signal from being drowned:

  • L-5: an injected fault is not an error, because if the fault modes fill the error log, the error log becomes noise and stops being read.
  • E-9: scheduled maintenance is a state, not a fault, because "a system that goes red every night at 2am teaches its operators to ignore red."

Both are Goodhart defenses. An alarm that fires when nothing is wrong is not a cautious alarm; it is a broken one, and its brokenness is invisible right up until the night it was needed.

A bug you cannot reproduce is a rumor

You cannot fix a rumor. You can only change something and then fail to observe the symptom — which feels exactly like fixing it, and is the most common way a bug gets "fixed" twice.

Reproduction is what converts an anecdote into an object you can hold still, take apart, and verify you have actually changed. So the first move on any bug report is never a hypothesis. It is a reproduction.

This is what the T requirements are actually buying:

  • T-1 bans unseeded randomness — deterministic sequences, exact asserted values — so a failure is the same failure tomorrow.
  • T-2 drives faults through the control plane instead of waiting for them: set down, poll, assert unreachable, set ok, assert recovery. A fault you can cause on demand is a fault you can study.
  • T-5 returns the whole system to a known state in one command, so "it works here" and "it fails there" become comparable claims.
  • C-9 and L-4 require every derived state to record the inputs that produced it, so a failure in production arrives already carrying its own reproduction.

Notice that all four are about lowering the cost of the next investigation, not about this one. That is the shape of nearly all good engineering investment.

5.2 The team is a first-order variable

Four reasons, each with a consequence you can act on.

The system will resemble the team. Conway observed it in 1968: a system's structure copies the communication structure of the organization that built it, because an interface between two components can only be as well-specified as the conversation between the two people who own them. So the component table in parking/README.md is also a team structure. A README per component, stating its contract, is what two people agree on so that they can stop talking. If the team does not choose its shape, the system inherits whatever shape happened — usually "who sat together."

Knowledge lives in heads, and heads leave. Everything that is K0 to exactly one person is K0 with no backup. A teammate takes a job, gets sick, drops the course, and the deletion is uncontrolled. parking/ is about not having single points of failure — one key, one chain, one clock — and the team building it has one in whoever understands the signing string.

The team's fear sets its practice. SOFTWARE-ENGINEERING.md describes the avoidance gradient: fear rises near the deadline and destroys exactly the practices that would have made the deadline survivable. That makes team discomfort an engineering input with a leading-indicator property. It shows up weeks before the schedule does, and somebody going quiet in stand-up is data.

Coordination cost grows faster than headcount. Four people are six pairs; six people are fifteen. Every pair needs an interface, and the interfaces nobody wrote down get negotiated in meetings. That is the whole mechanism behind "adding people to a late project makes it later," and it is why the fix is always a smaller, written interface rather than another meeting.

The consequence of all four: the team's shape is a design decision with the same status as the schema. It is made early, it is hard to change, and everything built afterward assumes it. Make it on purpose, write it down, and version it when it changes.

A team fails the way an input fails

The essays say every input a component depends on needs a stated answer for four cases: absent, late, wrong, and lying. A teammate is an input. A plan that only works when everyone is present, on time, right, and candid is the team version of a system that only works when its inputs behave — not finished.

5.4 The interface between two people is a contract

SOFTWARE-ENGINEERING-2.md makes the argument — the interface between two developers is the same object as the interface between two modules. This is the procedure.

Before splitting work, write the contract between the pieces and check it in. Then both people start on the same day.

  1. Name the seam. "I'll build the collector, you build the journal" names two components and no seam. The seam is the append call: its shape, its errors, what happens when the journal is down.
  2. Write the shape. Signature, schema, error codes. contract/README.md says why it exists — so that two people can build the sensor and the collector without talking to each other — and it is layer 0 for the same reason. An hour, done properly.
  3. Write the mock that misbehaves on request. A journal that accepts, a journal that refuses, a journal that hangs. The other person's component now has something to be built against.
  4. Write the test that fails today and passes when the real thing arrives.
  5. Integrate every day. A mock is a K4 — the real thing will behave like this — and daily integration is how you find out when it came due. Mock everything, integrate at the end, and every person delivers on time into a system that does not work.

Meetings are the tax on unwritten interfaces. If the same meeting keeps happening on the same topic, an interface is missing. Write it, and the meeting stops.

The proportion mismatch

The client measures a requirement by its size in their world: spaces, dollars, headcount, minutes. The design measures it by how many assumptions it invalidates. These two sizes have nothing to do with each other. A requirement covering one space in five hundred can be most of a redesign. A requirement that sounds enormous — "also show the lots on a map" — can be a line in Non-goals that costs nothing at all.

Neither party is wrong. The gap between the two senses of proportion is where K2 lives, and closing it is the team's job, not the client's — because only the team knows what the assumptions are.

  • "We're assuming nobody has a claim on a space before their car is in it. True?"

Each one converts a K4 — or a K3 you did not know you were holding — into K1 for the price of one sentence. Do it in week one, and again every time the scope decisions change. The client will answer most of them in two seconds, and one of them will make them stop and say "well, except…"

5.6 The first conversation

Ninety minutes, in week one, at the site if there is one. Order matters: pain before solution, people before features, numbers before nouns.

  • What happens today when it goes wrong? Who gets the call? What do they say?
  • Who are all the kinds of people this touches? Is any of them treated differently?
  • Walk me through a bad day from your side. Show me the clipboard, the radio, the spreadsheet.
  • How many? And in two years? (E-1 through E-4)
  • How long may it be down, and when? (E-6)
  • What are we explicitly not building?
  • Who is affected who is not in this room?
  • What would you do differently with this answer?
  • Read the K4s back.

The second item is the one teams skip, and section 5.7 is what it costs. It is not a features question. It is a taxonomy question — a census of everyone the system touches — and its answers arrive as nouns you have never heard: season-ticket holders, the VIP gate, the tow contractor, the ADA row. Each noun is a candidate for the glossary and a candidate for a requirement, and the client will not volunteer any of them, because in their world those are not "the app."

Output, within forty-eight hours: REQUIREMENTS_v1. Numbered, observable, with the numbers in — invented ones marked invented. A Scope decisions list and a Non-goals list. A glossary at the top, ten to thirty nouns, one line each. A Not yet answered section. Sent to the client with one sentence attached: tell us what is wrong with it.

Specific and wrong beats vague and safe. The first draft's job is to be wrong in a way that is cheap to correct, and the only way it can do that job is by making claims precise enough to be rejected.

In parking/. The E section is arithmetic done on the client's answers, not a transcript of them. "Counts, not locations" is a scope decision with an ethical reason attached. "The gate staff radio each other and guess" is the answer to what happens today, and it is why the requirements are about trust in a number rather than about a map. What is not there is a glossary — and section 5.7 is where its absence is felt.

5.7 The iteration

One to two weeks. Ends at a fence. Produces four things, and an iteration that produces three is not done:

  1. A running thing the client can touch, from a clean reset, on data shaped like theirs.
  2. A diff to REQUIREMENTS — v(n) to v(n+1) — if anything changed, including Non-goals.
  3. A FINDINGS entry: what was learned at full price this iteration.
  4. A QUEUE that says what is next, priced, with a done sentence on each item.

Thin vertical slice first, then thicken. parking/ iteration one: one lot, one sensor with its fault switchboard, the collector polling it, the driver page showing live, stale, and unknown. Signed readings, because the signing string is layer 0 and the bridge should carry the load-bearing idea from the start. Not: eight languages, firmware, the log chain. Enough to set a sensor stuck in front of the client and watch what they say.

Demo rules

  1. The real system, from T-5's one-command reset. Not slides, not a video, not a branch.
  2. The client drives. Watch their hands and their face, not the screen.
  3. Write down every "huh," "oh," "can it…," and "what about…" verbatim. Do not answer in the room. Those are K2 signals, and they are costed afterward, against the assumptions, not against the code. Section 5.7 is one of them.
  4. Break something in front of them. Set a sensor stuck. Their reaction to the stale display is a requirement, and it is one they could not have stated in advance.
  5. Read Non-goals aloud. Every demo. It is the cheapest way to find out that a non-goal is not one.
  6. Thirty minutes. Attention is the scarce resource on their side too.

Status between demos

Weekly, three lines, the same shape every time: done, next, risk-and-since-when. The uniformity is deliberate — when every status has the same shape, the one that deviates is a signal. Bad news goes out within a day of being known, with a number and an option attached, and never waits for the weekly.

5.8 The pivot: "It does the VIP spaces too, right?"

The first casualty is a word

available. To the sensor it means physically free. To a driver it now means free and not held. One word, two things — the silent error SOFTWARE-ENGINEERING-2.md §4 warns about, and the kind the types will not catch. Before any code moves, Shape settles the vocabulary:

  • available keeps its meaning. S-1 is untouched; the sensor never learns that VIP exists.
  • zone — a part of a lot with a class and a capacity. Today every lot has one zone. VIP lots have two.
  • hold — a claim on one space in a zone, by a holder, for a bounded time. It is either held, arrived, expired, or released.
  • holder — the person a hold belongs to. The fifth identity in I-1.

Three new nouns and one protected old one. That is the whole pivot at the level where it is cheap.

Team dynamics of the pivot

  • It is nobody's component, so it is Shape's. A change that cuts across every README belongs to the role that holds the whole, or it gets four local solutions and a merge conflict about what available means.
  • The temptation is to add a person. Resist it for the reason in section 5.1. The work is coherence, and coherence does not parallelize.
  • There is no blame to assign, and it is worth saying so out loud. The FINDINGS entry says findable by X; X is now on the list. It does not say who should have asked.
  • The client will feel they should have mentioned it. Say: "You did mention it. We didn't ask." Then move to the options. A client who feels blamed for a K2 stops volunteering the next one, and the next one is the expensive one.

5.9 Disagreement, bad news, and being behind

Disagreement

Four moves, in order. Most arguments end at the first.

  1. Is it a category dispute? Ask what requirement would have to disappear for this complexity to go away. One person is arguing taste and the other is arguing the problem; the question tells you which, and it converts a dispute with no end into a question with an answer.
  2. Set the intensity by reversibility. A one-way door — a schema with data under it, a published interface, a distributed key — gets the whole team and a written decision. Everything else gets decided by whoever holds the relevant role, with the reason written down and a revisit date named. Most arguments in software are conducted at the wrong intensity for their reversibility.
  3. If it is still split and cheap to test, spike both. One afternoon each, against the same test. That moves the question to K0 for the price of an afternoon instead of arguing it as two competing K3s for a week.
  4. Disagree in the conversation, not in the commit. Once decided, the decision goes into Scope decisions with its reason, and reopening it requires new information — not a new mood.

Bad news

The delay, not the slip, is the cost. A two-week slip found in week two is a planning problem; the same slip found in week eleven is a crisis; the difference is entirely the reporting delay. So the rule is mechanical: within a day of knowing, to the person it affects, with a number and an option.

"We're a week behind on the collector. We can drop the log chain from this iteration or slip the demo a week. We recommend dropping the chain."

Bad news with an option attached is planning. Bad news without one is a confession, and it makes the receiver do the planning under worse conditions than you had.

The same rule applies inside the team. The person who says "I'm stuck" on day one has cost the team a day. The person who says it on day five has cost five, plus the four days everyone else built on the assumption that the thing was coming.

Being behind

A person who is behind is a queue near full utilization, and queues do not degrade gracefully near full — latency goes to infinity. Three things follow:

  • Do not add work to the person with the most K0. That is the instinct, and it is the bus factor getting worse in real time.
  • Redistribute, pair, or cut scope. In that order, and say which in stand-up, with the since when.
  • It is a systems failure with a schedule, not a character flaw. Treat it as the second and you guarantee the first happens again, silently, because nobody will report it early.

The most dangerous team member is the one carrying the project. Rotate the roles anyway. Pair them with the person who knows least. The point of the term is four people who can each explain the whole thing, and a hero produces the opposite.

5.10 Agents on the team

An agent is a teammate with no memory and no stake. It arrives every morning knowing nothing that is not in the repository, and leaves every evening remembering nothing it learned. Everything in section 5.3 about written interfaces therefore applies twice: an agent cannot be caught in the hallway, and the constraint that lives only in a head is one the agent will violate cleanly, with tests, before lunch.

  • The client never talks to the agent. The Client role does, and what the client said reaches the agent only through REQUIREMENTS. Anything left in a chat log or a notebook does not exist as far as the agent is concerned — and, by the rule in section 5.2, did not happen.
  • The pivot, with an agent. Ask for the VIP feature and you will get the flag in five minutes: clean, tested, plausible, and carrying every line of the hidden bill in section 5.7. The costing table is the human's job and it happens before the prompt. What the agent is good for is the grep-shaped half of that job — "which assumptions in REQUIREMENTS does this sentence invalidate?" — because that is search, and search is what it is for. Then people decide.
  • Ask it the retro questions. What did you assume because nobody wrote it down? What did you have to read that you should not have needed? Put the answers in the repository. That is how an agent joins the team: not by remembering, but by the team writing down what it would have needed to remember.
  • Working code nobody can explain is a liability, and an agent produces it faster than a team can review it. The four roles do not change. Fence still owns the cost of a test; Shape still says no; and the signature on the commit is still a person saying I have read this and I stand behind it.

6.6 The system we are imagining

A stadium complex runs a hundred parking lots holding a hundred thousand spaces. Each lot has hardware that counts free spaces. Those counts accumulate into a mobile web app that tells a driver where to park right now — while everyone else is trying to park at the same time.

This document specifies observable behavior: what the system must do, stated so that it stays true no matter how we build it. Mechanism — keys, formats, algorithms — lives in DESIGN.md. Repository policy lives in ../CONTRIBUTING.md.

Requirement IDs (S-1, C-2, …) are stable. Reference them from tests, reviews, and commits.

6.7 Why this layer exists

Everything downstream is easy when sensors answer promptly and truthfully. They will not. Building this layer with faults and attacks we can switch on forces every consumer to answer questions it would otherwise skip:

  • What do we show when we cannot reach a sensor?
  • What do we show when we reached it, but the reading is old?
  • What do we do when it reports something impossible?
  • What do we do when it reports something plausible that it was not entitled to say?

A system that only works when its inputs behave is not finished. That is the lesson; the parking app is the excuse.

6.8 Scope decisions

Recorded so they are not relitigated:

  • One sensor per lot — not per zone, not per space. Per-zone sensing (a lot with one dark zone, where capacity is known but the count is not) is a richer problem and deliberately out of scope: it splits attention away from the failure-handling goal.
  • Counts, not locations. A sensor reports how many spaces are free, never which ones.
  • The collector lives in the existing Express app, not a service of its own. Splitting it out is a later lesson, not a starting condition.
  • One sensor image, many containers, differing only by environment.
  • Push-based firmware updates through the control plane. Real fleets pull because they sit behind NAT; we do not, and pulling would cost a service we do not need.
  • A single signing key, not a rotation hierarchy. Rotating a trust anchor using only the old anchor is genuinely hard and is named as deferred rather than pretended to be easy.

6.9 E — Operating envelope

How many, how badly may it break, and how long may it be down. The client answered these, and every requirement below is read inside these numbers. They are K4 — true enough now, false eventually — so they are written down where they can be checked rather than assumed by whoever is typing.

Scale

E-1. One hundred lots, one hundred thousand spaces, one sensor per lot. The mean lot holds a thousand spaces; no requirement may assume a lot is small. One sensor now speaks for a thousand spaces, which makes the Scope decisions bet larger than it was and S-6 — capacity disagreement is a fault, not a correction — more expensive to get wrong.

E-2. Load is an event, not a steady state. Roughly a hundred thousand vehicles arrive over the two hours before an event (~14 a second across the complex) and leave in under one (~28 a second). Arrival is not spread evenly across lots: a favored lot can fill in ten minutes, which is 1.7 vehicles a second into a single count. Sizing is against the peak; the daily average is not a design input. Load is event-shaped; availability (E-6) deliberately is not.

E-3. Up to one hundred thousand clients may have the customer view open at once, and it must stay correct and fresh for all of them. How often a client asks is a design variable, and E-2 and E-3 together are what constrain it.

E-4. At one poll per sensor per five seconds, the collector ingests about twenty readings a second — 1.7 million a day, 630 million a year. Storage, indexing, and retention are sized from this number, not from the demo.

E-5. A count can be wrong while being fresh by the clock. At E-2 peak, a ten-second-old count for a filling lot is off by seventeen spaces. Staleness thresholds (N-3, U-2) are derived from the fill rate, not chosen as a round number.

Availability

E-6. Availability is set by the clock, not by the calendar. Outside 02:00–04:00 local, total user-visible unavailability of the customer view may not exceed five seconds in any 24 hours; inside that window it may reach one hour. An event does not buy a larger budget and a quiet Tuesday does not shrink one — which is the point: the system never has to know whether an event is happening. Five seconds out of the twenty-two is 99.994%; quote the figure for the whole day and it is 95.8%, which is why the window has to be named rather than folded into a number of nines.

E-7. A backend failure shorter than the budget must not be visible to the driver at all. The customer view rides out a brief gap — last good count, its age, no error screen — so recovery happens inside E-6 rather than consuming it. At five seconds a day, no human is in the loop: detection and failover are automatic, or the budget is spent before anyone has read the page.

E-8. The budget is one hour inside a two-hour frame, and the extra hour is rollback margin, not spare budget. Everything that cannot be done live happens here: schema migration (V-7), firmware rollout (G), and mechanical service on the lots. Anything that cannot be completed and verified — and if necessary reversed — inside the window must be doable live, or it cannot be done at all. Two consequences worth stating: the abort decision comes at 04:00 minus however long the rollback takes, so the rollback has to have been timed; and an hour is not long against E-4's row counts, so a migration that scans the reading table is not a migration this system can perform.

E-9. Maintenance is a state, not a fault. A lot under scheduled service is distinct from down, stale, and degraded: it does not alarm, does not count against E-6, and reads to a driver as closed for service rather than as missing data. A system that goes red every night at 2am teaches its operators to ignore red.

E-10. The window is stated in local time, and local time is not monotonic. Twice a year it jumps: one hour never happens, and one hour happens twice. So the frame is not always two hours wide — on the spring transition it holds a single real hour, exactly the budget, with the margin of E-8 gone — and an instant inside the repeated hour is ambiguous if window membership is decided by comparing formatted local clock strings. Membership is computed on a real timeline (N), the transition nights are known in advance, and work that needs margin is not scheduled on the one night that has none.

  • How much data may be lost — in minutes of readings — when the database is restored?

7 The Working Agreement

Working agreement for AI agents contributing to this repository. Repository mechanics (how to run things, what the code does) live in CLAUDE.md.

7.1 Who owns this

Humans own the structure. Agents author and advise.

The humans here are computer science students, and their goal is fluency: being able to read, explain, change, and defend every part of this system without help. An agent that produces working code a student cannot explain has made the repository worse, not better. Correctness is necessary; comprehension is the point.

The test for a change is not "does it work." It is "can a student read this next week and say why it is here."

Everything an agent writes is a draft until a human accepts it. Make rejection cheap: small diffs, one concern at a time, nothing bundled in that wasn't asked for.

7.2 Write for human attention

Attention is the scarce resource in this repo — not tokens, not runtime, not lines of code.

  • Smallest change that does the job. Solve the problem in front of you. Related improvements you noticed are worth a sentence at the end, not a diff.
  • Boring over clever. When two versions work, ship the one that needs less explanation.
  • No speculative structure. No interface with one implementation, no config value that is never varied, no helper called once, no layer added for a requirement nobody has stated.
  • Dependencies cost attention. Each one is something a student must learn, trust, and audit. Prefer what is already in package.json and the standard library. Adding a dependency is a proposal, not a decision.
  • Keep the seams visible. A student should be able to follow a request from browser click to fetch to route to SQL to response by reading the code. Indirection that hides that path has to earn its place.

7.3 Comments

  • Comment the why: a constraint, a trade-off, a gotcha, something that surprised you. The what is already in the code.
  • Do not narrate obvious lines. A comment on every line trains students to stop reading comments.
  • Match the comment density of the file you are editing.
  • If code needs a paragraph to explain, first try to make the code need less explanation.
  • Design rationale and alternatives considered belong in the conversation, not in the file.

7.4 Limit your own fluency

You write code more fluently than this repository needs. Spend that fluency on finding the simple version, not on demonstrating range.

  • Write the version a second-year student would write, and would recognize a month later.
  • Three plain statements beat one dense chain.
  • Do not reach for an exotic language feature because it is tighter.
  • Do not optimize what is not slow. Note where it would matter, and move on.
  • Do not rename, reformat, or reorganize code you were not asked to touch. Unrequested diff noise is the fastest way to lose a reader's trust.

7.5 Advising

  • Give one recommendation and the reason it wins. If the call is genuinely close, say so in a sentence and still pick.
  • State plainly what you are unsure about.
  • Raise a real concern once, then do the work as asked. The human decides.
  • When the task is something a student is meant to learn: do it if asked, then say what to read and what to try next.
  • Never assert something about this code you have not opened the file to check.

7.6 Process

  • Propose before restructuring. Moving directories, adding a build step, or introducing a service is a conversation first.
  • Leave the repository runnable. If a change needs a manual step (a rebuild, a database reset), say so in the same breath.
  • Say what you did not do, and why. Silent omissions are worse than open ones.

9.1 The model

A shell is a small program that reads a line, splits it into words, runs the first word as a command, and hands it the rest as arguments. That is all. Every fancy thing it does is built on that one sentence.

you ──type──▶ terminal ──line──▶ shell ──runs──▶ program
                                   │                │
                          environment: $HOME,      stdin  ◀── keyboard, or a pipe
                          $PATH, $PWD, ...         stdout ──▶ screen, or a pipe, or a file
                                                   stderr ──▶ screen (even when stdout is redirected)
                                                   exit code: 0 means ok, anything else means not

Four things a program gets from the shell, and the fourth is the one people forget:

  • A working directory. pwd prints it. Every relative path is relative to it. Half of "no such file" is being in the wrong one.
  • An environment. Named strings, inherited by every program you start. $HOME is your home directory. $PATH is the list of directories searched for a command; "command not found" means the program is not in one of them. printenv shows them all.
  • Three streams. Input, output, and errors. | connects one program's output to the next's input. > sends output to a file; >> appends; 2>&1 folds errors into output so a pipe sees both.
  • An exit code. Zero is success. echo $? shows the last one. Continuous integration, npm run check, and every script in this repository use it as the idiot light: the number is the verdict, and the text is just for humans.

10 Git

Git is the version-control tool Linus Torvalds wrote to manage large federated projects like the Linux kernel. It is now used for far more than code: server configuration, documentation, and web content are all problems that benefit from a history you can go back to. In this course it is also the mechanism of ownership — a commit on main is signed by a person who has read it.

  • The fear it retires: "I can't get back to when it worked." And, signed: "nobody vouched for this code."
  • The fence you prove: ssh -T git@github.com greets you by name, and a commit you made shows Verified on GitHub.

  • After this: 1PASSWORD.md — you have just created two secrets that need a home

10.1 The model

A repository is a graph of snapshots. Each node is a complete state of the project — source, tests, build scripts, images — and each arrow points at what came before. A branch is a name for the newest node on one line of work. Merging joins two lines.

             iss32:  o──o──o
                    /        \
  main:  o──o──o──o───o───o───o──▶   (each o is a commit; a name like `main` points at the newest)

Locally, git keeps three things you move changes between, and most confusion is not knowing which one a command touches:

  working tree ──git add──▶ index (staged) ──git commit──▶ history (HEAD) ──git push──▶ remote (origin)
  the files you edit        the next commit's contents      what is recorded            GitHub's copy
                                                                           ◀──git pull──

Danger Will Robinson. Many tutorials show you how to make an isolated repository, and it is often a checkbox in an IDE. This is a bad idea, because there is safety in numbers, and you will be sad one day when your system does not boot and all your work is lost. A repository has a remote, or it is not finished.

Things to remember

  • Source goes in. Code, tests, build scripts, images, sounds, and the documents that say what the code is for.
  • Build artifacts, secrets, and minutiae stay out. Executables, libraries, node_modules, precompiled headers; API keys and passwords; the IDE's record of which windows are open.
  • Branches track issues, not people. db, ui, iss32 — not alice and bob. Bob might be sick and Alice has to step in. Git already records who made each change; the flow of work is about resolving issues.
  • Experiments are not on main. main is a sequence of merges from branches of code that compiles and passes more and more tests.

  • Git is an elephant. It never forgets. Once something is pushed, getting rid of it means rewriting history, force-pushing, and every collaborator re-cloning — and on a public repository, forks and caches mean you cannot be sure it is gone at all. So:

    • Do not git add . or use wildcard adds. You will add build artifacts that slow everyone down and create binary merge conflicts. Add files by name.
    • Do not add build artifacts. Large, useless to version control, and a conflict for everyone.
    • Do not add secrets. Use a password manager (1PASSWORD.md). If a secret does get pushed, rotate it first — revoke the key, issue a new one — before spending a minute scrubbing history, because the pushed value was compromised the moment it landed.
    • Do not add IDE minutiae. Removing them breaks the IDE; a conflict in them means you cannot use the IDE to fix it. A .gitignore says which files git should stop mentioning.

Most of this will not mean much until you have been bitten once. Read it again then.

D1. Tell git who you are. Your real name, and an email that is verified on your GitHub account; otherwise your commits will not link to your profile, which matters when someone looks you up to decide whether to interview you. git config --global user.name  "Jane Doe"

D2. Make a key pair. Press Enter through the prompts. If it says the key already exists, answer n — you do not want to overwrite the one you have.

ssh-keygen -t ed25519 -C "jane@example.com"

D3. Give GitHub the public half, twice. Copy it to the clipboard:

D3b. Check it. Before cloning anything:

ssh -T git@github.com

The first time it asks you to confirm GitHub's fingerprint; type yes. Success looks like a refusal: Hi jane! You've successfully authenticated, but GitHub does not provide shell access. If you see Permission denied (publickey) instead, go back to D3; every later command will fail in a more confusing way.

If it hangs forever, your network is blocking port 22 — some campus and corporate networks do. GitHub takes SSH over 443; put this in ~/.ssh/config:

Host github.com
  Hostname ssh.github.com
  Port 443

D4. Sign your commits. This repository requires it (CONTRIBUTING.md), and it is the same key. Four lines, once per machine:

git config --global gpg.format ssh
git config --global user.signingkey ~/.ssh/id_ed25519.pub
git config --global commit.gpgsign true
git config --global tag.gpgsign true

To verify signatures locally, git needs a list of trusted keys; this repository keeps one at .github/allowed_signers, and CONTRIBUTING.md shows the per-repository line that points git at it. Add yourself to that file in the format it shows. Then git log --show-signature -1 after your next commit should say Good "git" signature, and GitHub will show Verified beside it.

D5. Pick where projects live. ~/projects, per SHELL.md.

10.3 Once per repository

Two flavors. <user> is your GitHub username; <repo> is the repository name, in whatever convention your professor requires.

Clone, GitHub → local. Easiest, and right whenever the files do not already exist on your machine. Create the repository on GitHub (the + by your avatar), non-empty: include a README, a LICENSE, and a .gitignore for your language. Then, from your projects directory:

cd ~/projects
git clone git@github.com:<user>/<repo>.git

Push, local → GitHub. When the project already exists on your machine. Create the repository on GitHub empty — no README, no nothing. Then, inside the project directory:

git init .
git add <file1> <file2> ...        # by name; no wildcards
git commit -m "initial commit"
git branch -M main
git remote add origin git@github.com:<user>/<repo>.git
git push -u origin main

Use SSH, not HTTPS. HTTPS with a token is not insecure; it is friction, and friction is what kills the habit. The address from GitHub's Code button, SSH tab, looks like git@github.com:<user>/<repo>.git.

Two files in the first commit.

  • .gitignore — the patterns git should stop mentioning: node_modules/, build output, .env, the IDE's state. GitHub's language templates are a good start; this repository's is short and commented.
  • .gitattributes with one line, * text=auto. Windows ends lines differently from Mac and Linux, and in a mixed team that shows up as a diff claiming you changed all four hundred lines of a file you barely touched. This line makes git normalize line endings in the repository and hand each person the flavor their system expects.

Do not create a repository inside a repository. IDEs notice they are in a git-managed directory; if one offers to initialize a new one, say no.

10.4 Every day

See what changed. git status lists files changed since the last commit; git diff shows the changes; git diff --staged shows what the next commit will contain. If status keeps showing files you do not want, that is what .gitignore is for.

Add and commit. Name the files. Commit with a message that says why, and, in this repository, the requirement ID:

git add contract/signing.js contract/signing.test.js
git commit -m "S-8: refuse a field containing the separator"

This only affects the local repository. Almost always follow it with git push.

Push early. Push often. Your code takes up very little space and you will miss it if you lose it. A pushed commit is a backup you can get back to; on your own branch it is not a decision you have to defend.

Give and take. If you are not the only one pushing to a branch, sync the other way too: git pull.

Push rejected. Sooner or later: ! [rejected] main -> main (fetch first). Nothing is broken. The remote has commits you do not, because somebody else pushed or you pushed from another machine, and git will not let you overwrite what you have not seen. Bring theirs down, then send yours up: git pull, then git push. If git asks how to reconcile divergent branches, tell it once, globally, to merge: git config --global pull.rebase false. Never fix a rejected push with git push --force: it does exactly what it says and throws away commits you had not seen, which is somebody else's afternoon.

Branches

main is what GitHub creates now; older repositories say master. git branch tells you which you are in. It is easy to forget which branch you are on and branch from the wrong one, so start from main, fresh:

git switch main             # make sure you are on main   (older git: git checkout main)
git pull                    # get the latest from origin
git switch -c iss32         # create iss32 and switch to it (older git: git checkout -b iss32)
git push -u origin iss32    # publish it and remember the pairing; once per branch

git branch -a lists every branch, local and remote. Commit and push before switching branches. Git will sometimes let you switch with uncommitted changes sitting there, but "sometimes" is not a rule you can build a habit on, and half-switched work is how people lose an afternoon.

Deleting a branch is easy — git branch -d iss32 locally, git push origin --delete iss32 remotely — and usually unnecessary; a dead branch costs nothing. Deleting a commit that has been pushed is the elephant again.

Merging

Merging does not end either branch. To bring main into your issue branch, which you should do often so that your eventual pull request is clean:

git switch main && git pull        # get main current
git switch iss32                   # back to your work
git merge main                     # bring main in
git push

A good policy: merge main into the issue branch first; when the issue branch has all of main's changes and passes all the tests, then merge the issue branch into main — or, on a team, open a pull request, which is the same merge proposed rather than performed (GITHUB-ISSUES.md).

When a merge stops with a conflict

A conflict means two branches changed the same lines and git will not guess which you meant. It is a normal part of merging, not a sign something went wrong: the merge pauses and waits. git status lists the conflicted files under Unmerged paths, and those are the only files you touch.

Inside one, the disputed region is marked:

<<<<<<< HEAD
what your branch says
=======
what the branch you are merging says
>>>>>>> main

Make those lines read the way the file should finally read, and delete all three marker lines. In VS Code the file shows the two versions as colored blocks with links above them — Accept Current, Accept Incoming, Accept Both, Compare Changes — and the Source Control pane lists every conflicted file so you can watch the list shrink. For anything more tangled than a line, Compare Changes opens a three-way view that is much easier to think in than raw markers.

Read the result before you move on. Accepting one side is a decision about what the program should do, and the buttons make it fast enough to click through without reading. A file with both sides accepted often compiles fine and is still wrong.

When the file reads correctly: git add it to mark it resolved, repeat until nothing is unmerged, git commit (git writes the message), git push. Two escape hatches: git merge --abort puts you back exactly where you started, and git checkout --merge <file> restores the markers in one file if you mangled your edits. The first is the important one: you are never trapped in a merge.

11 1Password

You now own secrets: an SSH private key, two-factor recovery codes, and — the first time a project talks to any service — an API key. A secret has one property that nothing else you handle has: once it is in the wrong place, you cannot get it back. This is the tool that keeps secrets out of files, out of chat, and out of the repository, while still getting them into the programs that need them.

  • The fear it retires: "A secret is in the repo." And: "I'm locked out of the account with all my work in it."
  • The fence you prove: parking/ runs with JWT_SECRET injected by op run, nothing secret in any file, and your GitHub recovery codes are in the vault.
  • Before this: GIT.md
  • After this: GITHUB-ISSUES.md

Why this product: its developer tooling can put a secret into a program's environment at run time so that it never touches a file, it has an SSH agent, and it has not lost its customers' vaults. Do not use LastPass; its customer vaults themselves were stolen in the 2022 breach. Any manager with the first two properties will do; the habits below are the point.

11.1 The model

  vault ── items ── fields            the app (unlock with one password + a secret key)
    │                                  the browser extension (fills logins; generates passwords)
    │                                  the CLI, `op` (reads fields into a shell or a process)
    │                                  the SSH agent (signs with keys that never leave the vault)
    ▼
  repository: NO secrets.
  a template file: JWT_SECRET=op://Development/parking/jwt-secret     ← a reference, not a value
    │
    └── op run --env-file=.env.template -- npm start   ──▶  the process sees JWT_SECRET=<value>
                                                             and nothing on disk ever did

Three ideas:

  • A secret is a value that lives in exactly one place, the vault, and is referenced everywhere else. A reference like op://vault/item/field is safe to commit; it is an address, not a key.
  • The environment is the delivery route. Programs read secrets from environment variables (SHELL.md); the manager sets them for one process and nothing else sees them.
  • Your email account is the master key to everything else, because every "forgot password" flow goes through it. It gets the strongest password, hardware or app two-factor, and its own recovery codes in the vault.

11.2 Once per machine

  1. Install the app and the browser extension. Create the account with a long master password you will actually type, and save the Emergency Kit — the PDF with your secret key — somewhere that is not this machine. Without the secret key, a new device cannot join, and support cannot help you.
  2. Install the CLI (op), and turn on Settings → Developer → Integrate with 1Password CLI so that op unlocks with the app instead of asking for the password every time. op signin once, then op whoami to confirm.

  3. Turn on the SSH agent (Settings → Developer → Use the SSH agent) and either import the key you made in GIT.md into the vault as an SSH Key item, or generate a new one there and add its public half to GitHub. Then tell SSH to ask the agent:

    # ~/.ssh/config
Host *
  IdentityAgent "~/Library/Group Containers/2BUA8C4S2C.com.1password/t/agent.sock"   # mac
# linux: IdentityAgent ~/.1password/agent.sock

    ssh-add -l should now list the key, and ssh -T git@github.com should still greet you. On Windows the agent speaks through the OpenSSH named pipe, and using it from WSL takes a bridge; if that is friction, keep the key file in WSL and store a copy of the key in the vault as the backup it should have anyway.

  4. Move your existing secrets in. GitHub login and its two-factor recovery codes. Your email account and its codes. The SSH key. Anything currently in a notes file, a text message, or a screenshot of a QR code.

11.3 Once per repository

  1. .env is in .gitignore. Check before anything else. git status must never show it.

  2. Commit a template, not a value. .env.template (or .env.example) holds references:

    DATABASE_URL=postgres://parking_user:parking_pass@localhost:5432/parking
JWT_SECRET=op://Development/parking/jwt-secret

    The database line is not a secret here — it is the local Docker demo, and docker-compose.yml says so in plain text. The JWT secret is. That distinction is a decision; make it on purpose and write it down.

  3. Create the item. In the Development vault, an item named parking with a field jwt-secret, generated by the app, long, never typed by a human.

  4. Run through the manager.

    cd parking/app
op run --env-file=../.env.template -- npm start

    op resolves every op:// reference, sets the variables for that one process, and forgets them when it exits. op read "op://Development/parking/jwt-secret" prints one value when you need to see it, which should be rare.

  5. Share by vault, not by message. A teammate who needs the secret gets access to the shared vault. A secret pasted into chat is in that chat's history forever, on servers you do not control.

11.4 Every day

  • The extension fills logins and generates a new password for every new site. You should not know any of your passwords except the master one.
  • Watchtower tells you which saved passwords have appeared in a breach; rotate those.
  • When a project needs a new key: generate it in the service, paste it into a vault item once, reference it from the template, close the tab. It never goes in a file.
  • When a secret must be rotated — a teammate leaves, a laptop is lost — change the vault item and every process picks it up on its next start. That is the payoff for never copying it anywhere.

11.5 Danger Will Robinson

  • A secret that was pushed is compromised, however briefly it was there. Rotate it first — revoke, reissue — then, if you must, scrub history with git filter-repo, knowing that forks and caches may still have it.
  • .env in the repository. The single most common way a key leaks. The .gitignore line is not optional, and git add . is how it gets bypassed (GIT.md).
  • Secrets in a Docker image. ENV JWT_SECRET=… in a Dockerfile, or a build argument, is in the image's layers forever, and anyone who can pull the image can read it (DOCKER.md).
  • Secrets in a prompt. Pasting a key into a chat with an agent sends it to a third party (AGENT.md).
  • Secrets in logs. L-7 in parking/REQUIREMENTS.md: credentials, private keys, and tokens are never logged; fingerprints and subjects are.
  • One password reused. The breach of a site you forgot about becomes the breach of your email, and your email is the master key.

12 GitHub Issues and Pull Requests

Git records what changed. This is the layer that records why, who is on it, and whether anyone else looked. An issue is a unit of work; a branch is named for it; a pull request proposes the merge; a review approves it; the merge closes the issue. One loop, and every step leaves a record someone can find in a month.

  • The fear it retires: "Nobody knows what is being worked on, or why this change exists, or whether anyone read it."
  • The fence you prove: An issue closed automatically by a merged pull request that names it, with a review approval and a green check on it.
  • Before this: 1PASSWORD.md
  • After this: GITHUB-ACTIONS.md — the green check is what that one supplies

12.1 The model

  issue #32 ──▶ branch iss32 ──▶ commits ──▶ pull request ──▶ review + checks ──▶ merge ──▶ closed

Branches track issues, not people (GIT.md). The issue number is the thread that ties the branch, the commits, the pull request, and the conversation together, and Closes #32 in the pull request is what closes the loop by machine rather than by memory.

12.2 Once per repository

  1. Protect main. Settings → Branches → Add rule for main: require a pull request before merging, require status checks to pass, require signed commits, and do not allow force pushes. After this a direct push to main is refused no matter how correct the merge was, which is the point.
  2. Decide the merge method, once, in Settings → General → Pull Requests. Merge commits keep every commit and add one; squash collapses the branch into one commit; rebase replays them. Pick one so the history reads one way. Whatever you pick: merging in the web UI produces a commit signed by GitHub's key, not yours.   4. Create the labels you will actually filter by. Fewer is better; a label nobody filters by is noise.

12.3 Every day

Writing an issue

An issue is a what and a why, in a form that can be closed:

  • Title as a verb phrase. "Mark a lot stale when age_ms exceeds the threshold," not "staleness."
  • The pain, then the observable done criterion. What happens today, what must be true when it is fixed, which requirement ID. "Done when T-2's stuck-sensor test passes and the driver view shows the age."
  • Not a design. "Add a stale column" is a design; the issue says what must be observable, and the pull request says how (AGILE_VS_WATERFALL.md, section on requirement versus design).
  • A bug report is a reproduction. Exact steps, exact values, exact output. A bug you cannot reproduce is a rumor, and T-1 — deterministic sequences, exact asserted values — is what makes a reproduction possible to write.
  • Assign it when someone starts, not before. An issue with an assignee and no branch for a week is the stand-up's "blocked — since when."

Opening a pull request

git switch main && git pull
git switch -c iss32
# ... work, commit by name, push early ...
git push -u origin iss32

GitHub offers a Compare & pull request button right after the push. Fill the template. Put Closes #32 in the description. Mark it a draft if it is not ready for review; a draft says "look if you like, do not approve yet."

Keep it to one concern. Reviewer attention is the scarce resource, and a bundled diff spends it on separating things that should never have been joined. If the change touches two requirements, that is two pull requests. Merge main into your branch first (GIT.md) so the diff shows only your change.

Reviewing

The reviewer asks three questions, in order (CONTRIBUTING.md):

  1. Does it do what the requirement says, and does the test prove it?
  2. Could a student read this in a month and explain why it is here?
  3. What does it make harder later?

Comment on lines, not in general. Approve, Request changes, or Comment — and say which question the comment is about. As the author, respond to every comment, even with "done," so the reviewer can see the thread close. Push more commits to the same branch; the pull request updates.

Read the whole diff. The one line you skipped is the one that was generated, or pasted, or merged wrong. If you cannot explain a line, it does not go in; that rule applies to reviewers as much as authors.

Merging

When the checks are green and the review is approved:

git switch iss32 && git pull
git switch main && git pull           # main may have moved
git merge iss32                       # signed with your key
git push                              # the PR shows Merged; #32 closes

Then leave the branch alone or delete it; it costs nothing either way.

12.4 Danger Will Robinson

  • git push --force to a branch with an open pull request. It rewrites what the reviewer already read.
  • A pull request nobody can review in one sitting. Three hundred lines from an agent is not a pull request; it is a request that someone else take ownership of something you did not read.
  • Issues used as chat. Decisions go in the description or in the documents; a forty-comment thread is where decisions go to be lost.
  • Closing an issue by hand when the pull request should have. If it did not close, the description did not say Closes #32, and the link between the change and its reason is gone.

13 GitHub Actions

Continuous integration is the idiot light with a URL. Every push is built and tested on a machine that is not yours, and the result is one green or red mark on the pull request and one badge on the README. It retires two fears at once — "it works on my machine" and "nobody ran the tests" — and it is worth trusting only when it runs the same command you do.

  • The fear it retires: "It works on my machine." "Nobody ran the tests before merging."
  • The fence you prove: A green run on every push to your fork, running npm run check from parking/, with the badge in the README.
  • Before this: GITHUB-ISSUES.md
  • After this: DOCKER.md

13.1 The model

  event (push, pull_request, schedule, manual)
    └─▶ workflow   (.github/workflows/check.yml)
          └─▶ job(s)   (each on a fresh runner: a virtual machine GitHub starts and throws away)
                └─▶ steps   (a shell command, or an action someone published)
                      └─▶ log, and an exit code   ──▶  ✓ or ✗ on the commit, the PR, and the badge

Three things to hold onto:

  • The runner is a stranger's machine. Nothing on it is yours: no global installs, no .env, no ~/.ssh. Whatever the job needs, a step has to put there. That is the feature — it is what makes "works on my machine" impossible to hide.
  • The exit code is the verdict. A step that exits nonzero fails the job. npm run check is already shaped for this (SHELL.md).
  • The workflow file is code, in the repository, reviewed like anything else. Changing it is a pull request.

13.2 Once per repository

Create .github/workflows/check.yml. This is the workflow CONTRIBUTING.md describes for this repository, with the parts that exist today:

name: check

on:
  push:
    branches: [main]
  pull_request:
  workflow_dispatch:            # a "Run workflow" button, for when you want to run it by hand

permissions:
  contents: read                # least privilege: this workflow never writes to the repository

concurrency:
  group: check-${{ github.ref }}
  cancel-in-progress: true      # a second push makes the first run's answer irrelevant

jobs:
  tests:
    runs-on: ubuntu-latest
    timeout-minutes: 10
    defaults:
      run:
        working-directory: parking
    steps:
      - uses: actions/checkout@v7
      - uses: actions/setup-node@v5      # use the current major; the log warns when one is retired
        with:
          node-version: 20
      - run: npm run check                # the same command you run: layering rule, then tests

  signatures:
    runs-on: ubuntu-latest
    timeout-minutes: 5
    if: github.event_name == 'pull_request'
    steps:
      - uses: actions/checkout@v7
        with:
          fetch-depth: 0                  # the whole history, so the range below exists
      - run: git config gpg.ssh.allowedSignersFile .github/allowed_signers
      - run: |
          git log --format='%H %G?' origin/${{ github.base_ref }}..HEAD |
            awk '$2 != "G" { print "unsigned or untrusted: " $1; bad = 1 } END { exit bad }'

  integration:
    runs-on: ubuntu-latest
    timeout-minutes: 15
    defaults:
      run:
        working-directory: parking

      - uses: actions/checkout@v7
      - run: docker compose up --build -d

          for i in $(seq 1 30); do
            curl -fsS http://localhost:3000/api/lots > /dev/null && exit 0
            sleep 2

          docker compose logs; exit 1
      - run: curl -fsS http://localhost:3000/api/lots | head -c 400
      - if: always()
        run: docker compose down -v

  • Keep the workflow the same as your script. If the build step is ./build.sh in both places there is no second recipe to drift. A workflow full of steps that nobody runs locally is a workflow nobody can debug.

Platforms

Building for a platform and running on it are different tests. A matrix runs the same job on several runners, and fail-fast: false keeps the others going when one breaks, because the interesting question is always which platforms broke:

  tests:
    strategy:
      fail-fast: false
      matrix:
        os: [ubuntu-latest, ubuntu-24.04-arm, macos-latest, windows-latest]
    runs-on: ${{ matrix.os }}

ubuntu-24.04-arm is a real arm64 machine, which is how the container nuance in DOCKER.md gets tested rather than assumed.

Secrets in a workflow

Settings → Secrets and variables → Actions. A secret is exposed to a step only as ${{ secrets.NAME }}, is masked in the log, and is not available to pull requests from forks. Never echo one. Prefer not to need one: the tests in this repository need no secret at all, and that is a design property worth keeping.

13.4 Danger Will Robinson

  • pull_request_target with a checkout of the pull request's code. That event runs with the base repository's secrets; checking out and executing a stranger's branch under it hands them your secrets. Use pull_request unless you know exactly why not.
  • Untrusted text inside ${{ }} in a run: step. run: echo "${{ github.event.issue.title }}" is a shell injection; a title containing $(curl …) runs it. Pass such values through env: and quote the variable: env: TITLE: ${{ … }} then run: echo "$TITLE".
  • permissions: write-all, or no permissions: block at all, which can default to more than you meant.
  • An unpinned action. uses: someone/action@main runs whatever is on main today, as you. Pin a major at least; pin a commit SHA if the action is not from GitHub itself.
  • A path-filtered workflow made a required check. If it never starts for a commit outside its paths, the pull request reads pending forever.

14 Docker

A container is one machine, described in a file, that anyone can start. It retires the other half of "it works on my machine" — the half about what is installed — and it gives you the thing every test suite needs most: one command back to a known state.

  • The fear it retires: "It works on my machine." "I can't get back to a clean state."
  • The fence you prove: From a clean clone, docker compose up --build serves http://localhost:3000; docker compose down -v and up again gives the seed state; and you can say which architecture the image was built for.
  • Before this: GITHUB-ACTIONS.md
  • After this: PACKAGES.md

14.1 The model

  Dockerfile ──build──▶ image ──run──▶ container ──▶ a process in its own filesystem and network
  (a recipe)            (immutable,     (one running    │
                         layered)        copy; cheap;   ├── volume:  state that outlives the container
                                         disposable)    ├── port:    host:3000 → container:3000
                                                        └── network: compose makes `db` a hostname

Four nouns, and the mistakes are mostly confusing two of them:

  • An image is a snapshot of a filesystem plus a command to run. It is built from a Dockerfile, it is immutable, and it is made of layers so that a rebuild redoes only what changed.
  • A container is a running (or stopped) copy of an image. It is cheap and disposable. Anything written inside a container that is not on a volume dies with it.
  • A volume is where state lives on purpose: the database's files. parking/ has one, named pgdata. It survives down; it does not survive down -v.
  • A compose file describes several containers, their volumes, ports, environment, and the network in which they can find each other by service name. app reaches the database at db:5432 because compose made db a hostname.

14.2 Once per machine

  • Mac and Windows: Docker Desktop. On Windows it runs on the WSL 2 backend; turn that on in Desktop's settings, and run compose from the WSL side, in a project under the WSL home (SHELL.md — /mnt/c is slow, and for Docker's bind mounts it is very slow).
  • Linux: Docker Engine plus the compose plugin, from Docker's repository, not the distribution's older package. Add yourself to the docker group so sudo is not needed.
  • Check: docker run --rm hello-world, then docker compose version. Note the word: it is docker compose with a space; the hyphenated docker-compose is the old separate tool.
  • Know your architecture. docker version --format '{{.Server.Arch}}' prints arm64 on an Apple-silicon Mac and amd64 on most everything else. Remember which you saw.

14.3 Once per repository

A Dockerfile per service. parking/app/Dockerfile, annotated:

FROM node:20-alpine            # the runtime, pinned to a major: same policy as PACKAGES.md
WORKDIR /srv/app               # every later path is relative to here
COPY app/package.json ./       # dependencies first, so this layer is cached until they change
RUN npm install --omit=dev     # (should be: COPY the lock file too, and `npm ci` — see PACKAGES.md)
COPY app/ ./                   # then the code, which changes often
ENV NODE_ENV=production
EXPOSE 3000                    # documentation; compose does the actual port mapping
CMD ["npm", "start"]

The order matters. Docker caches each layer and reuses it when its inputs are unchanged, so copying package.json and installing before copying the source means an edit to index.js does not reinstall every dependency.

The build context. parking/docker-compose.yml sets context: . and dockerfile: app/Dockerfile, so the context is parking/, not parking/app/. That is why the COPY paths above start with app/. A Dockerfile cannot COPY from above its own context, and this layout lets the image copy in the shared libraries (contract/, identity/) once the service depends on them. Add a .dockerignore (node_modules, .git, .env) so the context sent to the builder is small and never contains a secret.

A compose file naming the services, from parking/docker-compose.yml:

services:
  db:
    image: postgres:16-alpine
    environment:
      POSTGRES_USER: parking_user
      POSTGRES_PASSWORD: parking_pass                # the demo database; not a secret, and labeled
      POSTGRES_DB: parking
    volumes:
      - pgdata:/var/lib/postgresql/data              # state, on a named volume
      - ./db/init.sql:/docker-entrypoint-initdb.d/init.sql:ro   # runs ONLY on an empty volume
    ports: ["5432:5432"]
  app:
    build: { context: ., dockerfile: app/Dockerfile }
    environment:
      DATABASE_URL: postgres://parking_user:parking_pass@db:5432/parking
      JWT_SECRET: demo-secret-change-me              # a demo value, in plain text, on purpose
      PORT: 3000
    depends_on: [db]
    ports: ["3000:3000"]
volumes:
  pgdata:

depends_on orders starting, not readiness: app may come up before Postgres accepts connections. The fix is a health check on db and condition: service_healthy on the dependency:

  db:
    healthcheck:
      test: ["CMD-SHELL", "pg_isready -U parking_user -d parking"]
      interval: 2s
      retries: 15
  app:
    depends_on:
      db:
        condition: service_healthy

That is the power-on self test from TESTING.md, applied to a dependency: do not start until the thing you need says it is ready.

Repeatable, almost

An image tag is a family, not a file. node:20-alpine on an Apple-silicon Mac pulls the arm64 build; on a GitHub runner it pulls amd64. Same Dockerfile, two architectures, two builds of every package inside. They behave the same almost always; native modules, packages with no arm64 build, numerical libraries, and anything timing-sensitive are where they differ. Three honest choices:

ChoiceCost
platform: linux/amd64 on the serviceemulation on Apple silicon: slow, and now the developer's machine is the odd one out

accept almost, and write down where it is load-bearingnothing today; a K4 with a known blast radius

Any of the three is fine. Not knowing which one you chose is not. And an image is only as repeatable as its inputs: the base tag floats to the newest 20.x, and npm install without a lock file resolves fresh every build. PACKAGES.md is the fix, and TESTING.md is the argument.

14.5 Danger Will Robinson

  • docker compose down -v on a database with real data. There is no undo. Back up first.
  • A secret in an image. ENV, ARG, or a copied .env lands in a layer, and layers are forever; anyone who can pull the image can read it. Secrets enter at run time, through the environment (1PASSWORD.md).
  • latest. An image tag with no version is a different image every week.
  • Running as root inside the container for a service that does not need it. Add a USER line.
  • A project under /mnt/c with a bind mount. Every file access crosses the boundary twice.

15 Packages

Every project stands on code you did not write, and that code changes under you. A package manager is how you say what you are compatible with, record what you actually installed, and take a fix when one arrives — three different jobs, and the mistakes come from giving all three to one file.

  • The fear it retires: "A different install broke it." And: "That CVE has been open for a year."
  • The fence you prove: A lock file committed, an install that refuses when the lock and the manifest disagree, and one dependency update taken as a pull request with green tests.
  • Before this: DOCKER.md
  • After this: AGENT.md

15.1 The model

  manifest  ──resolver──▶  lock  ──install──▶  node_modules/ (or .pixi/, or target/)
  what you are              what you ran:       what is on this machine right now
  compatible with:          exact versions,
  ranges, by major.minor    with hashes,
                            per platform
  committed                 committed           ignored

Semantic versioning is the interface between you and strangers: major.minor.revision, where the major changes when something breaks, the minor when something is added, and the revision when something is fixed. So the policy, from TESTING.md:

In the manifest, say what you are compatible with — the major, and the minor you need as a floor. In the lock, record what you ran — the revision, and the hash. Commit both.

the revision pinned: 4.19.2yes, by handsomeone edits every pin, everywhere, after reading every changelognever

Repeatability against survivability. The middle row survives; the last row is the one that is both.

15.2 Once per machine

Node. Install a version manager rather than a bare Node, so the version is per project: fnm or nvm. Then fnm install 20 (or nvm install 20). node --version should print a 20.x. The major is the pin; the revision floats.

pixi (or conda) for anything Python, R, or with a compiled dependency that varies by hardware: one installer from pixi.sh, then pixi --version. cargo comes with Rust via rustup. You will not need all three today; you will need the habit that is the same in all three.

15.3 Once per repository

package.json is the manifest. Ranges:

package-lock.json is the lock. It is generated, it is committed, and it is never edited by hand. Two commands, and the difference between them is the whole point:

CommandDoes
npm installresolves from the manifest, updates the lock, installs. For adding or changing a dependency.

Pin the runtime too: an engines field in package.json ("node": ">=20 <21") and a .nvmrc or .node-version file containing 20, so a teammate's version manager picks the same major.

In parking/app/, at this writing, the lock file does not exist, and app/Dockerfile runs npm install from the manifest alone. Every image build resolves express, jsonwebtoken, and pg fresh, and nothing records what it chose. The fix is three lines:

cd parking/app
npm install --package-lock-only        # write package-lock.json without touching node_modules
git add package-lock.json

then in app/Dockerfile, copy the lock beside the manifest and install with npm ci:

COPY app/package.json app/package-lock.json ./
RUN npm ci --omit=dev

parking/package.json, the workspace root, has no dependencies at all, on purpose: the tests use Node's built-in runner, so nothing has to be installed to run them. That is not minimalism; it is one fewer thing to learn, trust, audit, and update, forever.

pixi

pixi.toml is the manifest; pixi.lock is the lock, with one resolution per platform. The same policy, in its syntax:

[workspace]
platforms = ["osx-arm64", "linux-64", "win-64"]     # everything the lock will hold an answer for

[dependencies]

Reach for pixi instead of a container when a dependency differs by hardware, the toolchain is too big for an image, the tool has a real window, or it needs a device the container cannot see. The languages course repository's ml/ directory is the worked example and says when not to.

cargo

Cargo.toml and Cargo.lock; cargo build --locked is the npm ci. Same shape.

  • rm -rf node_modules && npm install as a fix. It works because the lock drifted or the manifest changed; find out which, or it will happen to the next person.
  • A dependency added by an agent. It is fluent about packages you have never heard of. Treat the addition as the proposal it is (AGENT.md).

16 Agents

An agent is an extremely fast writer with no stake in the outcome. It can read the repository, edit files, run commands, and draft a change in less time than it takes you to read one. Everything in this sequence so far — the shell, git, secrets, issues, checks, containers, packages — is what makes that safe to use, and the failure mode of agents is skipping it.

  • The fear it retires: "I could not have built this in the time I had." And the fear it creates, which this document is about: "working code nobody can explain."
  • The fence you prove: An agent-drafted change, reviewed line by line, run, signed by you with the Co-Authored-By trailer, green in CI, merged through a pull request — and you can explain every line of it to a teammate.
  • Before this: everything else in essentials/
  • After this: AGENTS.md and CONTRIBUTING.md, which are the working agreement this document sets up

16.1 The model

  you ──task, with a done sentence──▶ agent ──reads──▶ the repository: CLAUDE.md, the working set, code
   ▲                                    │
   │                                    ├──runs──▶ shell commands, tests, the container (if you allow)
   │                                    │
   └────── a diff, to review ◀──────────┘
   then: you read it, run it, sign it, own it.

Three facts decide how to work with one:

  • It arrives knowing nothing and leaves remembering nothing. Every session starts from the repository. Anything not written there — a constraint in your head, a decision in chat — does not exist to it, and it will violate that constraint cleanly, with tests, before lunch.
  • It is a K3 factory. Fluent, confident, plausible, and occasionally wrong is the precise shape of erroneous certainty, arriving faster than you can check it. "This should work" is not a run.
  • It is fastest at the bottom of the waterfall — code, tests, fixtures, migrations, the second and third test case — and no help at all at the top, where the work is conversations with people and laws. It will normalize a schema in a minute and never ask about the registrar. The requirement document is the railing.

16.2 Once per machine

SHELL.md told you to read a piped installer before running it. This is one; download it, read it, then run it. Start the agent inside a repository by typing claude there. It works in the terminal you already have, on the files git already tracks, and it asks before it edits a file or runs a command. Those prompts are the default mode, and you should stay in it until the habits below are habits.

16.3 Once per repository

  1. CLAUDE.md is what the agent reads at the start of every session. /init drafts one by reading the codebase; this repository's says mechanics — how to run things, where things live, what the seams are — and its first line points at AGENTS.md, the working agreement. Claude Code reads CLAUDE.md; to make it read AGENTS.md mechanically rather than by instruction, put @AGENTS.md in CLAUDE.md.
  2. AGENTS.md says who owns what: humans own the structure, agents author and advise, the smallest change that does the job, no speculative structure, dependencies as proposals, comment the why. It is the document you will most often quote back to the agent.
  3. The working set is in the repository. REQUIREMENTS, DESIGN, QUEUE, FINDINGS. This is the agent's memory. A constraint that lives only in a head is a K2 to the agent.

  4. Permissions, written down. .claude/settings.json in the project says what the agent may do without asking, and it is committed and reviewed like anything else:

    { "permissions": { "allow": ["Bash(npm test)", "Bash(npm run check)"] } }

    Allow the things you would run yourself a hundred times a day. Do not allow git push.

  5. One command to run and one to reset (DOCKER.md). An agent that cannot run the thing cannot check its own claim, and "never let confidence substitute for a run" needs a run to be possible.

16.4 Every day

The loop, and every step of it is one of the earlier essentials:

  1. Write the task as a done sentence, with the ID. "C-5: values outside [0, capacity] are rejected and the lot marked degraded; a test named C-5 fails without the change." Vague in, vague out. This is the acceptance criterion from AGILE_VS_WATERFALL.md, addressed to a machine.
  2. Ask for the smallest diff, one concern. "Propose before restructuring." "Show me the test failing first." "Do not touch files I did not name." The agent will happily produce three hundred lines; a diff you cannot review is a diff you cannot sign (GITHUB-ISSUES.md).
  3. Read the whole diff. Not the summary — the diff. If you cannot explain a line to a teammate, it does not go in. The line you skipped is the one that added a dependency, reformatted a file you did not ask about, or asserted nothing.
  4. Run it. npm run check, the container, the fault you meant to inject. The agent's "this should work" is not a run; yours is.
  5. Ask the retro questions. What did you assume because nobody wrote it down? What did you have to read that you should not have needed? Put the answers in the repository — CLAUDE.md, a README, FINDINGS — where the next session, human or not, will find them.

  6. Commit it yourself, with the trailer, and sign it.

    C-5: reject out-of-range readings at the boundary

Co-Authored-By: Claude <noreply@anthropic.com>

    The agent does not commit unless asked, and you should not ask. Your signature says I have read this and I stand behind it; the trailer says who drafted it. Both are true, and they are different claims.

  7. Open the pull request, and let the reviewer ask the three questions. The reviewer treats an agent-drafted change exactly like any other, which is the point of having reviewed it yourself first.

16.5 Danger Will Robinson

  • A secret in a prompt. The prompt goes to a third party. Reference by name, never paste a value (1PASSWORD.md).
  • Skipping the prompts.   • Letting it push. A push is a publication (GIT.md's elephant). It is yours to do, after reading.
  • Signing what you have not read. The signature is the whole mechanism of ownership in this repository. An unread diff under a good signature is worse than an unsigned one, because it spends the trust.
  • A test that names the ID and asserts nothing. T-3 can be gamed by a machine faster than by a person. Watch it fail first.
  • The five-minute flag. Asked for a feature that touches a scope decision, an agent will add a boolean column and a table and be done before you have opened the requirements. That is the pivot in docs/TEAM-AND-CLIENT.md at machine speed; the costing table comes before the prompt.

Appendix A — Glossary

Terms the three parts define in passing, collected. Each is one line here and argued at length where it first appears.

TermMeaning
K0 … K4The five kinds of not-knowing: the known; the known unknown; the unknown unknown; erroneous certainty; a falsehood kept on purpose. K0–K3 are in order of expense. K4 is a choice.
Observable behaviorSomething you could check from outside without reading the source. Requirements are written in it; "the code is clean" is not.
ContractThe promises one part of a system makes to another, written down: what it accepts, what it returns, and how it fails. Not the code that keeps them.
Working setREQUIREMENTS_vX, DESIGN_vX, QUEUE, FINDINGS — the four documents that make a project's state observable from where every party stands, versioned with the code.
FenceA checkpoint that proves something: a demo to a real client, a conformance run that exits nonzero, a deploy that happened. A status meeting is not one.
IterationA fixed period, one to two weeks, that ends at a fence: a running thing shown to the client, a diff to the requirements, a FINDINGS entry, a priced queue.
ClientThe person who can say yes, no, and how much. Their pain is evidence; their proposed solution is a guess.
Proportion mismatchThe client sizes a requirement by their world — spaces, dollars, minutes. The design sizes it by the assumptions it invalidates. The gap between the two is where K2 lives.
Read your K4sReading every scope decision and assumed falsehood back to the client as a question. Converts a K4 to a K1 at the price of one sentence.
Essential complexityDifficulty that belongs to the problem and survives every rewrite. Test: name the requirement that would have to disappear for it to go away.
Accidental complexityDifficulty you introduced. Invisible to you because you understand it; charged to strangers, and to you in a month.
Conceptual integrityOne coherent design over every good idea. It shows up first in vocabulary — one thing, one word; one word, one thing — and somebody has to hold it.
Locality of reasoningBeing able to be sure of a part without reading the whole. The real reason interfaces exist; reuse is a bonus.
CarryEverything a piece of software costs for as long as it exists: attention, review, upgrades, security surface, the chance it breaks a neighbor. Cost is build plus carry times lifetime.
CacheAny copy of state that might not match the original. Naming one inherits its questions: how stale, how would you know, and what happens to the user meanwhile.
IdempotentDoing it twice equals doing it once, so "did that go through?" stops being a question. "Set available to 37" is; "one car arrived" is not.
One-way doorA decision that cannot be cheaply undone: a schema with data under it, a published interface, a distributed key, a released image. Deliberate in proportion to it.
ProvenanceWho said this, and can I check it later without asking them again — for a reading, a log record, a firmware image, and a commit alike.
Designated liarA component built to fail or lie on request so the handling of it can be seen to work. In parking/, the sensor; after the pivot, the gate as well.
Operating envelopeThe client's numbers — how many, how badly it may break, how long it may be down — inside which every other requirement is read. Section E of the requirements.
Availability budgetTolerable downtime as a number with a period and a beneficiary. Unstated, it defaults to 100%, the most expensive requirement you can accidentally accept.
Thin vertical sliceThe bridge: click to route to SQL to response, built end to end first and then thickened. Horizontal layers all finish at the same time — never.
MockA stand-in that answers, and misbehaves on request. A K4 by construction: a scheduling risk converted into an integration risk, a good trade only if you integrate continuously.
Flat scheduleEverything startable at once, because every dependency between components has been written down as an interface, a mock, or a failing test rather than sequenced.
